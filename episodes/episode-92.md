## Episode 92: The Wooden Oxen and Sima Yi's Patience

In Chengdu, Emperor Liu Shan personally sees off Zhuge Liang's army as it marches north once more. At the fortress of Wugong, a Wei defector named Zheng Wen arrives at Wei Yan's camp, presenting the severed head of a Wei officer as proof of his sincerity. Wei Yan is delighted and takes Zheng Wen into his confidence. Based on intelligence from the defector, Wei Yan identifies what he believes is a weakness in Sima Yi's defenses and, ignoring Ma Dai's warnings, decides to bypass Longxi and strike directly at Beiyuan.

It is a trap. Zhuge Liang sees through Sima Yi's ruse of the false defector and dispatches Wang Ping and other officers to rescue Wei Yan. The Shu troops fight desperately and manage to break free of the encirclement. Zhuge Liang then orders Wei Yan and Ma Dai to challenge Sima Yi's forces daily, trying to lure him out of his fortifications, but Sima Yi refuses to take the bait. In desperation, Zhuge Liang sends Yang Yi to Sima Yi's camp bearing a letter of challenge and a set of women's clothing, mocking him for hiding behind his walls like a timid woman. Sima Yi is not only unruffled but sends back advice that Zhuge Liang should take better care of his health and reduce his worries.

As the stalemate drags on, Shu's grain supplies dwindle. Zhuge Liang orders his engineers to build three hundred "wooden oxen and flowing horses" (木牛流马), mechanical transport devices for hauling provisions across the difficult mountain terrain. Sima Yi sends his son Sima Zhao to intercept the supply convoys. The Wei forces capture several of the devices, and Sima Yi orders his own men to use them for Wei's supply lines.

**Historical significance:** This episode covers events from the Fifth Northern Expedition (234 AD), the final and longest campaign. The women's clothing insult is a famous scene from the novel, though historically what is recorded is that Zhuge Liang sent Sima Yi a woman's headdress and robe to provoke him. Sima Yi's refusal to engage is well-documented. He had concluded that Zhuge Liang's supply lines were his fatal weakness, and that patience would win the war without risking battle. The wooden oxen and flowing horses are mentioned in historical sources, including Pei Songzhi's annotations to the Sanguozhi (citing Zhuge Liang's collected works), though their exact design remains debated. They were likely sophisticated wheelbarrows or carts adapted for mountainous roads. The novel adds the dramatic detail of Wei capturing and copying the devices, which Zhuge Liang then exploits. Wei Yan's recklessness and friction with Zhuge Liang's other officers foreshadows the tragedy to come.
