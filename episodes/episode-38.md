## Episode 38: Zhou Yu Returns

Lu Su scolds Zhuge Liang for insulting Sun Quan, but Zhuge Liang calmly instructs Lu Su to tell the young lord that he has a strategy to defeat Cao Cao. Sun Quan's anger turns to curiosity. He agrees to convene a full war council the following day.

That night, Zhang Zhao visits Sun Quan privately and argues again for surrender, shaking Sun Quan's resolve once more. But Zhou Yu, who has been away commanding the frontier forces, races back to Chaisang upon hearing the news. As Wu's various officials visit Zhou Yu that evening, each pressing either for war or surrender, Zhou Yu remains deliberately ambiguous, revealing nothing. Finally, Lu Su brings Zhuge Liang to meet Zhou Yu. The two brilliant strategists size each other up carefully. Zhou Yu announces that in his view, war means certain defeat and surrender is the wiser course. Lu Su is horrified. But Zhuge Liang, reading the game, deliberately misquotes the "Ode to the Bronze Sparrow Terrace," claiming Cao Cao's true ambition in conquering the south is to seize the two legendary beauties, the Qiao sisters, one of whom is Zhou Yu's own wife. Zhou Yu, whether genuinely enraged or simply finding the provocation he needed, drops the mask.

The next morning, before the assembled court, Zhou Yu lays out the four critical military blunders Cao Cao has committed and declares that fifty thousand elite troops will be enough to destroy him. Sun Quan draws his sword, slashes the corner of his desk, and declares: anyone who speaks of surrender again will be treated like this table. He appoints Zhou Yu as Grand Commander of all Wu's forces.

**Historical significance:** Zhou Yu's return and his role as the architect of resistance is well attested in history. The Sanguozhi makes clear that Zhou Yu was the primary advocate for war and that Sun Quan's decision to fight was heavily influenced by his analysis. The "Bronze Sparrow Terrace" provocation, however, is a famous anachronism in the novel: the poem (铜雀台赋) was written by Cao Zhi after the Battle of Red Cliffs, not before, making Zhuge Liang's use of it a narrative impossibility that the novel employs for dramatic effect. The show faithfully reproduces this beloved scene. Sun Quan's sword-on-table declaration is from the novel. Historically, the decision to resist was real, but the theatrical gesture is dramatized.
