## Episode 81: Lu Xun Takes Command

Liu Bei's massive expeditionary force stretches across hundreds of li in linked encampments along the Yangtze. Advisor Ma Liang urges caution, pointing out that Lu Xun's repeated retreats are not cowardice but a deliberate strategy to wait for the right moment to strike. Liu Bei dismisses these concerns with contempt, boasting that he will grind Sun Quan into paste. He forbids Ma Liang from sending camp diagrams back to Zhuge Liang in Chengdu, seeing it as an insult to his own military judgment.

In Xu Chang, Sima Yi analyzes the situation for Cao Pi. He observes that Liu Bei's linked camps stretching four hundred li constitute a cardinal sin of military strategy, and predicts that if Lu Xun uses fire, the Shu army will be annihilated in a single battle. Sima Yi advises Cao Pi to prepare two forces: one to cut off Lu Xun's retreat and another to strike at Wu's undefended capital.

Meanwhile, in a secluded temple, Lu Xun has an audience with Sun Quan that will change the course of the war. Lu Xun lays out his three-part strategy: avoid the enemy's sharp edge, seize the initiative through patience, and destroy them when the moment comes. He declares that Liu Bei's eighty-thousand-strong army (inflated to hundreds of thousands in the drama's telling) is doomed because Liu Bei has become the very thing that guarantees defeat: a commander blinded by arrogance. Sun Quan, deeply impressed, orders an altar built overnight and formally appoints Lu Xun as Grand Commander of all Wu forces.

**Historical significance:** This episode sets the stage for the Battle of Yiling (夷陵之战, 222 CE), one of the three decisive battles of the Three Kingdoms period alongside Guandu and Red Cliffs. The historical Liu Bei's force was likely in the range of 40,000 to 50,000 troops, not the "800,000" of the novel's dramatic inflation. Lu Xun's appointment as overall commander at the young age of 39, over the heads of more senior Wu generals, was historically accurate and reflected Sun Quan's remarkable judgment of talent. Sima Yi's shrewd analysis of the linked camps, while dramatically embellished, captures the real strategic calculus that Wei was closely watching this conflict for opportunities to exploit.
