## Episode 73: The Death of Cao Cao

Lu Xun meets with Sun Quan and, having accomplished the Jingzhou campaign, requests to be relieved of the Grand Commander title and allowed to retire to study. This is characteristic Lu Xun: wielding power without clinging to it.

In Chengdu, the news of Guan Yu's death reaches Liu Bei, who collapses in grief. His anguish is total. In Xu Chang, Sun Quan sends Guan Yu's severed head to Cao Cao as a gift, attempting to redirect Liu Bei's rage northward. Cao Cao sees through the ploy instantly but nevertheless orders an honorable burial for Guan Yu, whom he has always respected as a warrior without equal. During the funeral, Cao Cao's chronic headaches flare with devastating intensity. His advisors urge him to summon Hua Tuo for treatment, but Cao Cao, paranoid to the end, refuses.

Sun Quan then urges Cao Cao to declare himself emperor. Cao Cao declines, recognizing it as a transparent attempt to make him a usurper and unite enemies against him. His condition deteriorates rapidly. On his deathbed, Cao Cao confronts his heir Cao Pi with a chilling question: did you murder your brother Cao Chong, the child prodigy I once intended as my successor? Cao Pi denies it. Whether satisfied or simply out of options, Cao Cao passes the mantle to Cao Pi and dies. With his death, an era ends. The man who unified northern China, who broke the old aristocratic order, who could quote poetry while planning massacres, is gone.

**Historical significance:** Cao Cao died on March 15, 220 CE, one of the most significant deaths in Chinese history. The scene where Sun Quan sends Guan Yu's head to Cao Cao is drawn from the novel, and the idea was indeed to frame Cao Cao for the killing. Cao Cao's headaches and his distrust of physicians are well-attested in historical sources, though the specific story about Hua Tuo varies between sources. The question about Cao Chong's murder is a dramatic invention; Cao Chong historically died of illness at age 12, though suspicions of foul play have circulated for centuries. Cao Cao's refusal to take the imperial title despite holding all real power is historical, and his famous line "if Heaven's mandate falls to me, let me be King Wen of Zhou" (letting his son be the one to formally establish a new dynasty) shows his political cunning even on his deathbed.
