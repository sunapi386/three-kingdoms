## Episode 93: Fire and Rain at Shangfang Valley

Facing a worsening spring famine, Zhuge Liang orders Wang Ping to raid Sima Yi's supply depots. The mission succeeds, and the Shu army's provisions are temporarily restored. Meanwhile, Emperor Cao Rui of Wei issues a decree stripping Sima Yi of his title of Grand Commander and demanding that he achieve a decisive victory over Shu within a month.

Under this pressure, Sima Yi commits 100,000 elite troops to a direct assault on Zhuge Liang's main camp at Qishan, hoping to draw the garrison out of Shangfang Valley to reinforce it. The plan works at first: Wei Yan leads his forces out of Shangfang Valley to relieve the besieged Qishan camp, and the Wei assault there collapses. But Sima Yi pivots and leads his army into the now-undefended Shangfang Valley, pursuing the retreating Shu defenders deeper inside. This is exactly what Zhuge Liang intended. He orders the valley's hidden stores of kindling and oil set ablaze, and Sima Yi's army is engulfed in an inferno. Sima Yi, recognizing that he is finished, tells his men they may surrender to spare their lives.

Then, at the moment of Zhuge Liang's greatest triumph, the heavens open. A sudden, torrential downpour extinguishes the flames and saves Sima Yi's army from annihilation. Watching from a hilltop, Zhuge Liang is devastated. This is the turning point of the entire series: the man who calculated everything could not account for the will of heaven.

**Historical significance:** The fire attack at Shangfang Valley is the most famous fictional episode of the later Northern Expeditions, entirely invented by the novelist Luo Guanzhong. There is no historical record of this event. In the novel, it serves a theological and narrative purpose: Zhuge Liang's line "Plans depend on man, but success depends on heaven" (谋事在人, 成事在天) encapsulates the tragedy of his entire life. He did everything right and still lost. Historically, the Fifth Northern Expedition was a prolonged stalemate at Wuzhang Plains in which Sima Yi simply refused to fight, correctly calculating that Zhuge Liang's health was failing and that time was on Wei's side. The miraculous rain is a narrative device drawn from Chinese literary tradition, where heaven intervenes to preserve the mandate of those destined to rule. It signals that the Shu cause, however righteous, is not fated to prevail.
