## Episode 84: The Death of Cao Pi and the Memorial on Dispatching the Troops

Ma Su travels to Wu and, through sharp diplomacy, persuades Sun Quan to maintain the Shu-Wu alliance against Wei. With the border secured, Shu's forces repel the Wei invasion on all fronts.

In Xu Chang, Cao Pi ignores Sima Yi's counsel and personally leads a force of 350,000 against Wu. The campaign unfolds exactly as Sima Yi predicted: the combined resistance of Shu and Wu crushes the Wei offensive, and Cao Pi nearly loses his life in the debacle. Chastened by this humiliation, Cao Pi finally does what he has resisted for years. He grants Sima Yi real military authority and charges him with guiding his son Cao Rui. Shortly after, Cao Pi's chronic illness flares and he dies.

Young Cao Rui ascends the Wei throne. The Cao clan loyalists, led by Cao Zhen and Cao Xiu, immediately urge the new emperor to neutralize the threat of Sima Yi. Cao Rui compromises: he banishes Sima Yi to a frontier garrison in the Yong-Liang region, far from the capital. But Sima Yi, rather than despairing, recognizes the opportunity. Guarding the western frontier means building an independent military base and cultivating personal loyalty among the troops. The seeds of the Sima family's eventual rise to supreme power are planted in this exile.

In Chengdu, the episode closes with Zhuge Liang composing the Memorial on Dispatching the Troops (出师表, Chu Shi Biao), one of the most famous documents in Chinese literature. In it, he lays out the case for the Northern Expeditions against Wei, invokes the memory of Liu Bei, and commits himself to the restoration of the Han dynasty or death in the attempt.

**Historical significance:** Cao Pi died in 226 CE at the age of 39. His decision to entrust Sima Yi with military power, paralleling Liu Bei's entrustment of Zhuge Liang, set the stage for the eventual Sima takeover of Wei decades later. Cao Rui (Emperor Ming of Wei) was actually a capable ruler who managed the Sima threat skillfully for his lifetime. The Memorial on Dispatching the Troops (227 CE) is one of the most celebrated pieces of Chinese prose. The saying "Reading the Chu Shi Biao without tears means one is not a loyal subject" (读出师表不下泪者，其人必不忠) has been repeated for centuries. The memorial is historically authentic. Sima Yi's "exile" to the frontier is dramatized but based on the real tension between the Sima and Cao factions at court.
