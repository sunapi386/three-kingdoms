## Episode 80: Lu Xun Takes Command

The aftermath of the battle at Fuchi is devastating for Wu. The veteran general Cheng Pu dies from arrow wounds, and Huang Zhong succumbs to his injuries shortly after. Liu Bei, grief-stricken but steeled by rage, vows to press forward until Sun Quan himself is destroyed. The Shu army advances toward Yiling, the last barrier before the Wu heartland.

Sun Quan, recognizing that his conventional commanders are outmatched, makes a bold decision: he appoints the young scholar-general Lu Xun as supreme commander of all Wu forces. It is a controversial choice. Lu Xun is young, bookish, and untested in independent command. But Sun Quan sees in him something his other generals lack: the strategic patience to fight a defensive war of attrition against an enemy driven by emotion.

Lu Xun immediately orders a general retreat and consolidation, abandoning forward positions to concentrate forces at Xiaoting. His commanders are furious. Officials petition Sun Quan to replace Lu Xun, accusing him of cowardice. Sun Quan refuses. Lu Xun's strategy becomes clear as Wu forces dig in at roughly fifty fortified positions along the river corridor. When Liu Bei launches assault after assault, the Wu defenders hold firm, inflicting heavy casualties. The Shu momentum stalls. Liu Bei, who expected a quick, cathartic war of vengeance, finds himself bogged down in exactly the kind of grinding campaign that favors the defender. The seeds of his catastrophic defeat at Yiling are being sown.

Meanwhile, in the Wu court, Fan Jiang and Zhang Da arrive with Zhang Fei's severed head, describing how they killed him. Sun Quan examines the head and declares, admiringly, "What a fierce Zhang Fei!" He then orders the two assassins seized, recognizing that men who murder their own lord are useful to no one and trusted by no one.

**Historical significance:** Lu Xun's appointment as commander is one of the great turning points of the Three Kingdoms era. Historically, Lu Xun (陆逊, 183-245 CE) was indeed seen as too young and scholarly by the veteran Wu generals, who resisted his authority. His strategy of strategic retreat and patient defense is well-attested and represents a textbook case of trading space for time against a numerically superior but overextended enemy. The death of Cheng Pu at Yiling is fictional; the historical Cheng Pu died years earlier, around 210 CE. Sun Quan's handling of Fan Jiang and Zhang Da varies between sources; in some accounts he sends them back to Shu in chains as a peace gesture, while in others he executes them. The stage is now set for the Battle of Xiaoting (夷陵之战), where Lu Xun's fire attack will destroy Liu Bei's army and effectively end Shu's ability to project power beyond its mountain borders.
