## Episode 95: Sima Yi Fakes Illness and Takes Control of Wei

The final episode leaps forward in time. In the great hall at Xuchang, Emperor Cao Rui's health is failing. He summons Sima Yi and appoints him Grand Commandant, a prestigious title that effectively strips him of actual military power. In private, Sima Yi and his companion Jingshu (a character created for the drama) share their feelings honestly. Sima Yi decides to marry her, and she is deeply moved. The dying Cao Rui tests Sima Yi by offering him full regency over his young heir, watching to see if Sima Yi will reveal his ambition. Sima Yi declines the offer, voluntarily resigns all his positions, and even arranges for his old military allies to be removed from their posts to ease Cao Shuang's suspicions.

Cao Rui dies and is succeeded by the boy emperor Cao Fang, with Cao Shuang as regent. In the Sima household, Jingshu dies in a difficult childbirth, and the grief-stricken Sima Yi suffers a stroke. Cao Shuang, convinced that Sima Yi is now a broken old man, grows complacent and plans a grand procession to the Gaoping Tombs with the emperor and the full court.

The moment Cao Shuang and the emperor leave the capital, Sima Yi reveals the truth. The illness was feigned. The frailty was performance. He mobilizes his hidden forces, seals the gates of Luoyang, secures an edict from the Empress Dowager denouncing Cao Shuang's faction, and takes control of the military and the government in a single morning.

The series closes with a title card: after the coup, the Sima family dominated Wei's politics for decades. Sima Yi's son Sima Zhao conquered Shu. His grandson Sima Yan deposed the last Wei emperor, founded the Jin dynasty, and conquered the Kingdom of Wu. The three kingdoms became one.

**Historical significance:** This episode dramatizes the Incident at the Gaoping Tombs (高平陵之变), which occurred on February 5, 249 AD, one of the most consequential coups in Chinese history. The historical details are largely accurate: Sima Yi did feign illness for approximately two years to lull Cao Shuang into complacency, and he did strike the moment Cao Shuang left the capital with the emperor. Cao Shuang surrendered after being promised his life would be spared, only to be executed along with his entire clan shortly afterward. The character of Jingshu is fictional, invented for the drama to humanize Sima Yi and give his patient scheming an emotional dimension. By ending the series with Sima Yi rather than with the fall of Shu or Wu, the show makes a bold narrative choice: the true protagonist of the Three Kingdoms era was not the noble Liu Bei, not the brilliant Zhuge Liang, but the man who simply outlasted them all. The final title card compresses roughly 30 years of history (249 to 280 AD) into a single sentence, covering the fall of Shu Han in 263, the founding of Jin in 265, and the conquest of Wu in 280. The message is stark: loyalty, brilliance, and righteousness all lost to patience and ruthlessness.
