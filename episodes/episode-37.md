## Episode 37: Tongue Battle with the Scholars

Cao Cao orders his armies to rest and refit while Cai Mao trains the Jingzhou naval forces on Poyang Lake, preparing for a final push south to destroy Sun Quan. In Jiangling, Liu Bei's position is precarious. Then Lu Su arrives from the Southland, ostensibly to pay respects to the late Liu Biao. Zhuge Liang sees the opportunity immediately and volunteers to accompany Lu Su back east to persuade Sun Quan to ally against Cao Cao.

On the journey, Lu Su repeatedly warns Zhuge Liang: "Whatever you do, do not mention how large Cao Cao's army is." Zhuge Liang smiles and agrees. Lu Su enters Sun Quan's court first, where he finds the elder statesman Zhang Zhao already leading the surrender faction, arguing that Cao Cao's million-strong host cannot be resisted. Sun Quan wavers. Lu Su delivers the decisive blow in private: if the officials surrender, Cao Cao will still employ them to govern the south, but if Sun Quan himself surrenders, he will be caged in the imperial capital of Xuchang for the rest of his life.

The next day, Lu Su escorts Zhuge Liang into the audience hall. In the outer corridor, more than twenty of Wu's sharpest scholars and advisors wait to challenge him. What follows is the legendary "tongue battle with the scholars" (舌战群儒). One by one, Zhang Zhao, Gu Yong, Yu Fan, Lu Ji, and Luo Tong attack Zhuge Liang's positions, and one by one he demolishes their arguments. When Zhuge Liang finally meets Sun Quan in the inner hall, he employs a deliberate provocation, goading the young lord until Sun Quan storms out in fury.

**Historical significance:** Zhuge Liang's embassy to Sun Quan is historical. The Sanguozhi records that Lu Su advocated the alliance and that Zhuge Liang traveled east to persuade Sun Quan. However, the spectacular "tongue battle with the scholars" is a novel invention, one of the most celebrated rhetorical set pieces in Chinese literature. The historical Zhuge Liang did argue the case for alliance, but the dramatic sequential demolition of each opponent is fiction. Lu Su's private argument to Sun Quan about the difference between his officials' fate and his own is also from the novel, though it captures a genuine political reality: Sun Quan personally had the most to lose from surrender.
