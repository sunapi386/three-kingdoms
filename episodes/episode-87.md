## Episode 87: Tears for Ma Su

The aftermath of the Jieting disaster unfolds. Zhuge Liang must decide what to do with Ma Su, the man whose arrogance cost Shu its best chance at conquering Wei. Ma Su is brought before the chancellor in chains. The scene that follows is one of the most emotionally wrenching in the entire series. Zhuge Liang, weeping openly, orders Ma Su's execution. He reminds the assembled officers that the late Emperor Liu Bei had explicitly warned him that Ma Su talks bigger than he can deliver and should never be given major responsibility. By trusting Ma Su despite this warning, Zhuge Liang acknowledges that the true fault lies with himself.

Ma Su's head is displayed before the army for three days as a warning, then sewn back onto his body and given a proper burial with full honors. Zhuge Liang composes a memorial of self-reproach to Emperor Liu Shan, writing: "Your servant is a man of mediocre talent, unable to enforce discipline and the laws, resulting in the loss of Jieting." He requests that the emperor strip him of his rank. Liu Shan, uncertain how to respond, settles on a compromise: Zhuge Liang is demoted to Right General but retains all the actual powers of the chancellorship and supreme military command.

A rare bright spot emerges from the retreat. Zhao Yun and Deng Zhi return with their forces completely intact, having conducted a textbook withdrawal under pressure. Zhuge Liang is astonished and declares: "Zhao Zilong is truly a great general." He offers Zhao Yun a reward of surplus silk, but the old warrior refuses, saying the army failed and there is nothing to celebrate. This moment highlights the character of the last surviving Tiger General, now elderly but still embodying the values of duty and humility.

**Historical significance:** The execution of Ma Su is one of the few events in this stretch that is solidly historical. The self-demotion is also documented: Zhuge Liang voluntarily reduced his own rank by three grades after the failure, an extraordinary act of accountability that cemented his reputation for integrity. Zhao Yun's orderly retreat is also confirmed in the historical record (though the details are sparse). The real Zhao Yun was indeed praised for his disciplined withdrawal, and he did refuse rewards. Zhuge Liang's self-reproach memorial exists in historical texts and was not a novelistic invention.
