## Episode 61: The Trap at the Capital

Cao Zhi holds a celebratory banquet at his residence after his triumph at the Bronze Sparrow Terrace, where he has been rewarded with a noble title. His friend Yang Xiu astutely points out that Cao Cao granted the title not to honor Cao Zhi's literary talent but to reward his essay urging Cao Cao to accept imperial elevation. Cao Pi's essay, by contrast, contained language deferential to the Han dynasty, which displeased their father. Sima Yi visits Cao Pi and counsels patience, observing that the absence of key officials like the Xun clan from Cao Zhi's banquet proves that the loyal old guard still favors the Han court. Cao Pi confesses he had actually written a second essay urging his father's elevation, but held it back when he saw that the loyalist ministers stayed away. From this point on, Cao Pi and Sima Yi forge a close political alliance.

Meanwhile, the loyalist official Huang Kui approaches Miao Ze with a plan to open the northern gate and admit Ma Teng's troops into the capital to assassinate Cao Cao. Miao Ze weighs his options and ultimately betrays the conspiracy to Cao Cao, who lays an overnight ambush. Ma Teng's forces charge into the city and are caught in the trap. Ma Teng fights bravely but is captured and executed. When Huang Kui's confession implicates Cao Pi, Cao Cao orders his son seized. Cao Pi flatly denies everything and deflects blame onto Cao Zhi, narrowly escaping punishment.

**Historical significance:** Ma Teng's death in the capital is drawn from the novel's dramatization of events around 211 CE. Historically, Ma Teng had been summoned to the capital years earlier and was indeed executed by Cao Cao, but the circumstances were less theatrical. The show compresses and dramatizes the Ma Teng conspiracy to set up the succession rivalry between Cao Pi and Cao Zhi, which was a genuine and consequential political struggle in the state of Wei. The Bronze Sparrow Terrace poetry contest is a famous set piece from the novel, not recorded in the histories.
