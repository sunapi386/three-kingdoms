## Episode 71: The Trap Closes on Jingzhou

Sun Quan receives a letter from Cao Cao urging him to seize Jingzhou while Guan Yu is distracted. The Wu strategist Lu Xun proposes a diplomatic ploy: offer a marriage alliance between the Sun and Liu families to gain leverage over Jingzhou. Zhuge Jin travels to Jingzhou as matchmaker, but Guan Yu contemptuously rejects the proposal, declaring his daughter unfit to marry the "dog of Wu." This insult gives Sun Quan the pretext he needs.

Guan Yu, consumed by ambition, strips Jingzhou of its entire garrison to launch a massive assault on Fan Castle. His initial attack falters and he takes an arrow wound, but he devises a brilliant stratagem: flooding the Wei positions. The waters surge and Guan Yu wins a spectacular victory, capturing the Wei commander Yu Jin and killing the defiant Pang De. His arrow wound festers, however, and the legendary physician Hua Tuo arrives to perform the famous operation of scraping poison from the bone while Guan Yu calmly plays chess. The scene showcases Guan Yu's superhuman composure, but also his fatal flaw: he believes himself invincible.

Meanwhile, Lu Meng feigns illness, and Sun Quan appoints the young, seemingly harmless Lu Xun as acting Grand Commander. This is a ruse. Guan Yu, deceived into thinking Wu poses no threat, pulls his remaining Jingzhou reserves to the front. That night, Wu commandos seize the signal beacon towers along the river, and Lu Meng's main force advances unopposed into the heart of Jingzhou.

**Historical significance:** This episode compresses several chapters of the novel and several months of the historical Battle of Fancheng (219 CE) into a single dramatic arc. The flooding of Fan Castle (水淹七军) is one of Guan Yu's most celebrated victories and is attested in historical sources, though the novel dramatizes it considerably. Hua Tuo's bone-scraping surgery is almost certainly fictional; historians doubt Hua Tuo was alive at this point. Lu Meng's feigned illness and the "white-robed soldiers crossing the river" stratagem are historically documented and represent one of the most successful deception operations in Chinese military history. The loss of Jingzhou would reshape the entire strategic landscape of the Three Kingdoms, destroying Liu Bei's two-front capability and dooming Zhuge Liang's grand strategy.
