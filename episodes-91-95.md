# New Three Kingdoms (新三国) - Episodes 91-95
*The final chapter*

These five episodes bring the series to its conclusion, spanning the last years of Zhuge Liang's Northern Expeditions, his death at Wuzhang Plains, and Sima Yi's ultimate seizure of power in Wei. The arc traces a transfer of destiny: from the idealist who gave everything for a promise made to a dying king, to the patient schemer who outlived every rival and handed his descendants an empire.

---

## Episode 91: The Forged Letter

The armies of Shu and Wei clash on the battlefield, and Zhuge Liang deploys a formation so brilliant that Sima Yi is left speechless. The Wei troops charge in recklessly and are routed. Jiang Wei urges an immediate pursuit, but Zhuge Liang hesitates: the supply wagons have not arrived. The supply officer Gou An has been negligent, delaying provisions by several days. By rights the offense warrants execution, but Zhuge Liang stays his hand because Gou An is the nephew of Li Yan, one of the two regents entrusted by the late Emperor Liu Bei. He settles for a lesser punishment and sends Gou An back toward Chengdu.

On the road home, Gou An is captured by Wei soldiers and brought before Sima Yi, who sees an opportunity. He orders Gou An to carry a forged letter back to Shu designed to drive a wedge between Li Yan and Zhuge Liang. The scheme works. Li Yan presents the letter to Emperor Liu Shan and accuses Zhuge Liang of harboring treasonous ambitions. The alarmed young emperor orders Zhuge Liang to abandon his campaign and return to the capital immediately. Zhuge Liang complies, traveling back to Chengdu alone. In a private audience, Li Yan confronts him with the letter. Zhuge Liang calmly dismantles the forgery, exposing Gou An's betrayal. Liu Shan has Li Yan stripped of rank and imprisoned, reduced to a commoner. In a gesture that astonishes the court, Zhuge Liang then recommends Li Yan's son, Li Feng, for the post of Director of the Imperial Secretariat. With the crisis resolved, Zhuge Liang prepares to return to the front lines at Qishan.

**Historical significance:** This episode dramatizes events from Zhuge Liang's Fourth Northern Expedition (231 AD). The historical Li Yan (also known as Li Ping) was indeed one of two regents appointed by Liu Bei on his deathbed, alongside Zhuge Liang. In the historical record, Li Yan failed to deliver supplies due to heavy rains and then tried to cover his failure by shifting blame onto Zhuge Liang, claiming the retreat was unauthorized. Zhuge Liang produced Li Yan's own earlier correspondence proving the deception, and Li Yan was exiled. The forged-letter plot involving Gou An and Sima Yi is a dramatic invention from the novel "Romance of the Three Kingdoms," but the underlying power struggle between Zhuge Liang and Li Yan, and the supply crisis that forced a withdrawal, are historical. Zhuge Liang's magnanimity toward Li Yan's son is attested in the records and speaks to his reputation for principled governance. Li Yan reportedly died of grief in 234 upon hearing of Zhuge Liang's death, having held out hope that Zhuge Liang would eventually recall him from exile.

## Episode 92: The Wooden Oxen and Sima Yi's Patience

In Chengdu, Emperor Liu Shan personally sees off Zhuge Liang's army as it marches north once more. At the fortress of Wugong, a Wei defector named Zheng Wen arrives at Wei Yan's camp, presenting the severed head of a Wei officer as proof of his sincerity. Wei Yan is delighted and takes Zheng Wen into his confidence. Based on intelligence from the defector, Wei Yan identifies what he believes is a weakness in Sima Yi's defenses and, ignoring Ma Dai's warnings, decides to bypass Longxi and strike directly at Beiyuan.

It is a trap. Zhuge Liang sees through Sima Yi's ruse of the false defector and dispatches Wang Ping and other officers to rescue Wei Yan. The Shu troops fight desperately and manage to break free of the encirclement. Zhuge Liang then orders Wei Yan and Ma Dai to challenge Sima Yi's forces daily, trying to lure him out of his fortifications, but Sima Yi refuses to take the bait. In desperation, Zhuge Liang sends Yang Yi to Sima Yi's camp bearing a letter of challenge and a set of women's clothing, mocking him for hiding behind his walls like a timid woman. Sima Yi is not only unruffled but sends back advice that Zhuge Liang should take better care of his health and reduce his worries.

As the stalemate drags on, Shu's grain supplies dwindle. Zhuge Liang orders his engineers to build three hundred "wooden oxen and flowing horses" (木牛流马), mechanical transport devices for hauling provisions across the difficult mountain terrain. Sima Yi sends his son Sima Zhao to intercept the supply convoys. The Wei forces capture several of the devices, and Sima Yi orders his own men to use them for Wei's supply lines.

**Historical significance:** This episode covers events from the Fifth Northern Expedition (234 AD), the final and longest campaign. The women's clothing insult is a famous scene from the novel, though historically what is recorded is that Zhuge Liang sent Sima Yi a woman's headdress and robe to provoke him. Sima Yi's refusal to engage is well-documented. He had concluded that Zhuge Liang's supply lines were his fatal weakness, and that patience would win the war without risking battle. The wooden oxen and flowing horses are mentioned in historical sources, including Pei Songzhi's annotations to the Sanguozhi (citing Zhuge Liang's collected works), though their exact design remains debated. They were likely sophisticated wheelbarrows or carts adapted for mountainous roads. The novel adds the dramatic detail of Wei capturing and copying the devices, which Zhuge Liang then exploits. Wei Yan's recklessness and friction with Zhuge Liang's other officers foreshadows the tragedy to come.

## Episode 93: Fire and Rain at Shangfang Valley

Facing a worsening spring famine, Zhuge Liang orders Wang Ping to raid Sima Yi's supply depots. The mission succeeds, and the Shu army's provisions are temporarily restored. Meanwhile, Emperor Cao Rui of Wei issues a decree stripping Sima Yi of his title of Grand Commander and demanding that he achieve a decisive victory over Shu within a month.

Under this pressure, Sima Yi commits 100,000 elite troops to a direct assault on Zhuge Liang's main camp at Qishan, hoping to draw the garrison out of Shangfang Valley to reinforce it. The plan works at first: Wei Yan leads his forces out of Shangfang Valley to relieve the besieged Qishan camp, and the Wei assault there collapses. But Sima Yi pivots and leads his army into the now-undefended Shangfang Valley, pursuing the retreating Shu defenders deeper inside. This is exactly what Zhuge Liang intended. He orders the valley's hidden stores of kindling and oil set ablaze, and Sima Yi's army is engulfed in an inferno. Sima Yi, recognizing that he is finished, tells his men they may surrender to spare their lives.

Then, at the moment of Zhuge Liang's greatest triumph, the heavens open. A sudden, torrential downpour extinguishes the flames and saves Sima Yi's army from annihilation. Watching from a hilltop, Zhuge Liang is devastated. This is the turning point of the entire series: the man who calculated everything could not account for the will of heaven.

**Historical significance:** The fire attack at Shangfang Valley is the most famous fictional episode of the later Northern Expeditions, entirely invented by the novelist Luo Guanzhong. There is no historical record of this event. In the novel, it serves a theological and narrative purpose: Zhuge Liang's line "Plans depend on man, but success depends on heaven" (谋事在人, 成事在天) encapsulates the tragedy of his entire life. He did everything right and still lost. Historically, the Fifth Northern Expedition was a prolonged stalemate at Wuzhang Plains in which Sima Yi simply refused to fight, correctly calculating that Zhuge Liang's health was failing and that time was on Wei's side. The miraculous rain is a narrative device drawn from Chinese literary tradition, where heaven intervenes to preserve the mandate of those destined to rule. It signals that the Shu cause, however righteous, is not fated to prevail.

## Episode 94: The Chancellor's Death at Wuzhang Plains

Atop the hill overlooking the failed fire attack, Zhuge Liang collapses, coughing blood. In his tent at the Shu camp, gravely ill and knowing his time is short, he entrusts the future of the state to Jiang Wei and Yang Yi. Wei Yan forces his way into the tent against the guards' objections. Zhuge Liang, in a calculated final act, pretends to hand over supreme military command to Wei Yan, who accepts. As soon as Wei Yan leaves, Zhuge Liang secretly summons Ma Dai and orders him to eliminate Wei Yan when the time comes.

In the twelfth year of Jianxing (234 AD), Zhuge Liang dies at Wuzhang Plains, his lifelong dream of restoring the Han dynasty unfulfilled. Before his funeral altar, Wei Yan seizes the commander's seal and orders the army to continue the Northern Expedition. Ma Dai strikes, killing Wei Yan in front of the altar, and command passes to Yang Yi. The Shu army begins its withdrawal south, following Zhuge Liang's final instructions.

Sima Yi orders his forces to pursue in three columns. But at the Chencang road crossing, the Wei army rides into an ambush. Jiang Wei wheels out a wooden effigy of Zhuge Liang mounted in his signature four-wheeled chariot. Sima Yi, terrified that Zhuge Liang might have faked his own death as a trap, panics and flees with his entire army. The Shu forces withdraw safely to Hanzhong. This incident gives rise to the famous saying: "A dead Zhuge scares away a living Sima" (死诸葛吓走活仲达).

Back in Wei, Emperor Cao Rui orders Sima Yi to surrender his military authority to Xiahou Ba and report to the capital. Sima Yi departs without protest, but his soldiers chase after him, swearing their eternal loyalty. The seeds of the Sima family's future power are already planted.

**Historical significance:** Zhuge Liang's death at Wuzhang Plains in the autumn of 234 AD (the eighth month of Jianxing 12 by the lunar calendar) is one of the most significant events in Three Kingdoms history. He was 53 years old. His final words and arrangements are among the most retold scenes in Chinese literature. The killing of Wei Yan is partly historical and partly dramatized. In the novel, Zhuge Liang prophesied Wei Yan's rebellion and arranged his death in advance. Historically, the situation was more ambiguous: after Zhuge Liang died, Wei Yan and Yang Yi each accused the other of treason, and Wei Yan was killed by Ma Dai during the resulting power struggle. Many historians believe Wei Yan was treated unjustly and that Yang Yi manipulated events. The wooden effigy ruse and Sima Yi's panicked retreat appear in the novel but have a basis in the historical accounts, which record that the Shu army's orderly withdrawal did indeed startle Sima Yi into retreating. The "dead Zhuge scares a living Sima" proverb became one of the most enduring expressions in Chinese culture.

## Episode 95: Sima Yi Fakes Illness and Takes Control of Wei

The final episode leaps forward in time. In the great hall at Xuchang, Emperor Cao Rui's health is failing. He summons Sima Yi and appoints him Grand Commandant, a prestigious title that effectively strips him of actual military power. In private, Sima Yi and his companion Jingshu (a character created for the drama) share their feelings honestly. Sima Yi decides to marry her, and she is deeply moved. The dying Cao Rui tests Sima Yi by offering him full regency over his young heir, watching to see if Sima Yi will reveal his ambition. Sima Yi declines the offer, voluntarily resigns all his positions, and even arranges for his old military allies to be removed from their posts to ease Cao Shuang's suspicions.

Cao Rui dies and is succeeded by the boy emperor Cao Fang, with Cao Shuang as regent. In the Sima household, Jingshu dies in a difficult childbirth, and the grief-stricken Sima Yi suffers a stroke. Cao Shuang, convinced that Sima Yi is now a broken old man, grows complacent and plans a grand procession to the Gaoping Tombs with the emperor and the full court.

The moment Cao Shuang and the emperor leave the capital, Sima Yi reveals the truth. The illness was feigned. The frailty was performance. He mobilizes his hidden forces, seals the gates of Luoyang, secures an edict from the Empress Dowager denouncing Cao Shuang's faction, and takes control of the military and the government in a single morning.

The series closes with a title card: after the coup, the Sima family dominated Wei's politics for decades. Sima Yi's son Sima Zhao conquered Shu. His grandson Sima Yan deposed the last Wei emperor, founded the Jin dynasty, and conquered the Kingdom of Wu. The three kingdoms became one.

**Historical significance:** This episode dramatizes the Incident at the Gaoping Tombs (高平陵之变), which occurred on February 5, 249 AD, one of the most consequential coups in Chinese history. The historical details are largely accurate: Sima Yi did feign illness for approximately two years to lull Cao Shuang into complacency, and he did strike the moment Cao Shuang left the capital with the emperor. Cao Shuang surrendered after being promised his life would be spared, only to be executed along with his entire clan shortly afterward. The character of Jingshu is fictional, invented for the drama to humanize Sima Yi and give his patient scheming an emotional dimension. By ending the series with Sima Yi rather than with the fall of Shu or Wu, the show makes a bold narrative choice: the true protagonist of the Three Kingdoms era was not the noble Liu Bei, not the brilliant Zhuge Liang, but the man who simply outlasted them all. The final title card compresses roughly 30 years of history (249 to 280 AD) into a single sentence, covering the fall of Shu Han in 263, the founding of Jin in 265, and the conquest of Wu in 280. The message is stark: loyalty, brilliance, and righteousness all lost to patience and ruthlessness.
