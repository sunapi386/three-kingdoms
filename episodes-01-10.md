# New Three Kingdoms (新三国) - Episodes 1-10
*The fall of the Han and rise of Dong Zhuo*

These episodes cover the opening arc of the 2010 television series directed by Gao Xixi (高希希), starring Chen Jianbin as Cao Cao, Yu Hewei as Liu Bei, Peter Ho as Lu Bu, and Ni Dahong as Sima Yi. Unlike the 1994 CCTV adaptation, this version opens not with the Peach Garden Oath but with Cao Cao already in Dong Zhuo's court, establishing him as the series' central figure from the first scene.

---

## Episode 1: Cao Cao Presents the Sword (曹操献刀)

In the final years of the Eastern Han dynasty, the warlord Dong Zhuo has seized control of the imperial capital. He dominates the court through brute force, with his adopted son Lu Bu serving as his personal champion. The aging Grand Preceptor Wang Yun and the other senior officials are in despair, powerless against Dong Zhuo's tyranny.

Cao Cao, serving as a minor military officer (校尉), has been outwardly cooperative with Dong Zhuo while secretly harboring plans for his removal. At a birthday banquet hosted by Wang Yun, Cao Cao arrives uninvited and makes bold declarations about eliminating the tyrant. Wang Yun, outraged by the perceived recklessness, expels him from the gathering. But later that night, Wang Yun privately summons Cao Cao to an inner chamber, where Cao Cao lays out his assassination plan. Moved to tears, Wang Yun presents Cao Cao with his family's ancestral seven-star blade to aid in the deed.

The next day, Cao Cao gains an audience with Dong Zhuo under the pretext of a private meeting. Just as he draws the blade, Dong Zhuo catches sight of him in a mirror and turns. Thinking fast, Cao Cao drops to one knee and claims he has come to present the precious sword as a gift. Dong Zhuo examines the blade with suspicion, but before he can press further, Cao Cao excuses himself and flees the capital at a gallop, smashing through checkpoints along the way. When Dong Zhuo realizes the assassination attempt was real, he sends Lu Bu in hot pursuit.

**Historical significance:** The "presenting the sword" incident is drawn from Chapter 4 of the novel *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* and has no firm basis in historical records. In actual history, Cao Cao simply fled the capital after Dong Zhuo consolidated power, then raised troops in his home region. The novel and this adaptation use the scene to establish Cao Cao as bold and cunning from the very start. The series' decision to open here rather than with the Yellow Turban Rebellion or the Peach Garden Oath signals its focus on political intrigue and Cao Cao as protagonist.

## Episode 2: Chen Gong Releases Cao Cao (陈宫义释曹操)

Back in the capital, Wang Yun and the other ministers initially celebrate, believing Cao Cao has succeeded. When word arrives that Dong Zhuo is alive and furious, the tyrant issues a nationwide warrant for Cao Cao's arrest: anyone who delivers his head will be rewarded with the title of Marquis of Ten Thousand Households. Wang Yun, crushed by the failure, retreats home and attempts to take his own life. His adopted daughter Diao Chan rushes to stop him, comforting the despairing old man.

Lu Bu leads troops to search Wang Yun's residence for any trace of Cao Cao. During the search, he catches sight of Diao Chan for the first time, and the two exchange lingering glances, planting the seed for later events.

Meanwhile, in a separate narrative thread, Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei swear their famous oath of brotherhood in a peach garden, pledging to live and die together. This scene, compressed into a brief sequence in this adaptation, sets up their bond without dwelling on their humble origins.

Cao Cao, fleeing east, is captured in Zhongmou county by the local magistrate Chen Gong. Chen Gong initially intends to turn Cao Cao in for the bounty, but during a late-night conversation in the jail cell, he becomes deeply impressed by Cao Cao's vision and courage. The more they talk, the more Chen Gong admires this fugitive's ambition to save the dynasty. By dawn, Chen Gong has abandoned his post, his wife, and his comfortable life to join Cao Cao on the road.

**Historical significance:** Chen Gong's release of Cao Cao is a famous episode from the novel (Chapter 4), though historical sources are sparse on their early relationship. Historically, Chen Gong was a real figure who later became Lu Bu's chief strategist. The Peach Garden Oath (桃园结义) is entirely fictional but has become one of the most iconic scenes in Chinese culture, symbolizing loyalty and brotherhood. This adaptation treats it almost as an aside, reinforcing how the show prioritizes the Cao Cao storyline over the traditional Liu Bei focus of the novel.

## Episode 3: The Murder at Lu Boshe's Estate (曹操误杀吕伯奢)

Cao Cao and Chen Gong are now wanted fugitives, their faces posted on notices throughout the region. As night falls, they seek shelter at a remote estate belonging to Lu Boshe, an old sworn brother of Cao Cao's father. The elderly Lu warmly welcomes the pair and insists on riding out to buy wine to properly entertain his honored guests.

After Lu Boshe departs, Cao Cao and Chen Gong hear the sounds of blades being sharpened and servants shouting in the courtyard. Cao Cao, paranoid and on edge, concludes that Lu Boshe has gone to alert the authorities and that the servants are preparing to kill them. He and Chen Gong burst out and slaughter everyone in the household, only to discover that the servants had been preparing to butcher a pig for dinner. It was hospitality, not treachery.

Horrified, the two flee into the night. On the road, they encounter Lu Boshe himself, returning cheerfully on his donkey with wine. Cao Cao, knowing the old man will discover the massacre and raise the alarm, draws his sword and kills him too. Chen Gong is appalled. Cao Cao delivers the line that will define his character for the rest of the series: "I would rather betray the entire world than let the world betray me" (宁教我负天下人, 休教天下人负我).

Rain begins to fall. In a chilling coda, Cao Cao suggests they return to the estate to eat the meal the servants had prepared, reasoning that the dead should not have died for nothing. He performs a solemn offering to Lu Boshe's spirit, blaming not himself but the chaos of the age. The next morning, Chen Gong quietly slips away, unable to remain with a man capable of such ruthlessness.

**Historical significance:** This episode dramatizes one of the most debated incidents in Three Kingdoms lore. Historical sources (the *Wei Shu*, *Shiyu*, and *Sun Sheng's Miscellaneous Records*) give contradictory accounts of what happened at Lu Boshe's home. The novel synthesized these into the version shown here. The famous quote about betraying the world appears in the novel but is not found in any historical record. The scene serves as a moral turning point: it reveals the cold pragmatism that will make Cao Cao both great and terrifying. Chen Gong's departure sets up their eventual reunion as enemies, one of the series' most powerful later arcs.

## Episode 4: Guan Yu Slays Hua Xiong (温酒斩华雄)

After parting ways with Chen Gong, Cao Cao travels to Chenliu, where he raises an army using his family's wealth. He then sends out a call to arms, summoning the lords of the realm to form a coalition against Dong Zhuo. Eighteen regional warlords answer, assembling a grand alliance under the nominal leadership of Yuan Shao, the most aristocratic among them. The alliance gathers at Hulao Pass, where Dong Zhuo's forces are entrenched.

Dong Zhuo's champion general Hua Xiong proves devastatingly effective, cutting down several coalition commanders in single combat. Yuan Shu's officer Yu She is killed. The warlord Han Fu sends his boasted champion Pan Feng to face Hua Xiong, only to have Pan Feng slain in a single exchange. Panic spreads through the allied camp as no one dares volunteer to fight next.

At this point Guan Yu, an unknown warrior serving the equally obscure Liu Bei, steps forward. Yuan Shu mocks him as a mere foot soldier, but Cao Cao intervenes, noting that Guan Yu has the bearing of a hero and pouring him a cup of warm wine as encouragement. Guan Yu rides out, and before the wine has cooled, he returns with Hua Xiong's severed head. The feat stuns the assembled lords and establishes Guan Yu's legend. Cao Cao is further impressed and privately tells his cousin Cao Ren that he intends to recruit the three brothers. In a conversation with Liu Bei that foreshadows the famous "heroes over green plum wine" scene many episodes later, Cao Cao and Liu Bei share their mutual disdain for the coalition's incompetent lords.

**Historical significance:** "Slaying Hua Xiong while the wine is still warm" is one of the most celebrated scenes in the novel and in Chinese popular culture. Historically, however, it was Sun Jian (not Guan Yu) who defeated Hua Xiong. The novel reassigned this feat to Guan Yu to glorify the Liu Bei faction. The eighteen-lord coalition against Dong Zhuo is historical (190 AD), though the novel exaggerates its unity and drama. The coalition actually fell apart quickly due to internal rivalries, which even this dramatized version hints at through the petty squabbling between Yuan Shao and Yuan Shu.

## Episode 5: Three Heroes Battle Lu Bu (三英战吕布)

The coalition's internal dysfunction deepens. Yuan Shu, entrusted with supplying grain to the vanguard led by Sun Jian, deliberately withholds provisions out of jealousy. When Yuan Shao confronts him, Yuan Shu smugly explains his "strategic reasoning." Before the argument can be resolved, reports arrive that Sun Jian's starving troops have been routed by Dong Zhuo's forces.

Dong Zhuo personally leads the elite Western Liang cavalry to the front at Hulao Pass. His champion Lu Bu, mounted on the legendary Red Hare horse and wielding his crescent halberd, charges into the coalition lines and kills several generals in succession. The allied warlords watch in horror as none of their champions can last more than a few exchanges against Lu Bu's ferocity.

Zhang Fei, unable to contain himself, charges out with a roar and engages Lu Bu in single combat. The two fight fiercely but reach a stalemate. Guan Yu rides out to join his brother, and the two press Lu Bu hard. Finally, Liu Bei draws his twin swords and enters the fray. The three brothers surround Lu Bu in a whirling battle that is the series' first major action set piece, featuring ambitious choreography and special effects including Lu Bu firing eighteen arrows in rapid succession. Though outnumbered, Lu Bu fights to a standstill before withdrawing. The coalition forces seize the moment and surge forward, driving Dong Zhuo's army back.

Recognizing the threat the coalition poses, Dong Zhuo retreats to the capital and announces his intention to relocate the emperor and the entire court to the western capital of Chang'an, far from the coalition's reach.

**Historical significance:** The "Three Heroes Battle Lu Bu" (三英战吕布) is entirely fictional, invented by the novel to showcase the prowess of all four warriors. There is no historical record of such a duel. Lu Bu was indeed a formidable warrior allied with Dong Zhuo, but his historical defeats were in conventional battles, not single combat against three opponents. Dong Zhuo's decision to relocate the capital to Chang'an is historical (190 AD) and was one of the most destructive events of the era: Dong Zhuo's forces burned Luoyang, looted imperial tombs, and forced the population westward. The coalition's failure to prevent this marked its effective collapse.

## Episode 6: The Imperial Jade Seal (孙坚得玉玺)

Cao Cao borrows four thousand cavalry from Sun Jian under a pretext and rides ahead to pursue Dong Zhuo, who is forcibly marching the Emperor, the court, and the people of Luoyang westward to Chang'an. Dong Zhuo's generals Li Jue and Guo Si provide a rear guard, and an ambush in the mountain passes inflicts heavy losses on Cao Cao's pursuing force. During the chaos, Lu Bu abandons his duty protecting the imperial carriage to instead rescue Diao Chan, revealing where his true loyalties lie.

Sun Jian, accompanied by his sons Sun Ce and Sun Quan and his veteran officers Cheng Pu and Huang Gai, enters the ruined capital of Luoyang. In the wreckage of the great palace, they discover the legendary Imperial Jade Seal (传国玉玺), the symbol of heavenly mandate to rule, hidden within the imperial throne. Cheng Pu and Sun Quan explain its centuries-old history. Sun Jian is overcome with excitement at possessing it.

When the coalition lords reconvene in the ruins of Luoyang to celebrate their "victory," the atmosphere quickly sours. Cao Cao, Liu Bei, and Sun Jian each express disgust with the alliance's incompetence and self-interest. Yuan Shu accuses Sun Jian of secretly keeping the Jade Seal. A tense standoff follows, with both sides producing hidden soldiers. Sun Jian seizes Yuan Shao as a hostage, forcing him to publicly deny the accusation. The anti-Dong Zhuo coalition officially collapses, and each lord departs to pursue his own ambitions.

**Historical significance:** Sun Jian's discovery of the Jade Seal is from the novel; historical sources differ on who found it and when. The Seal's symbolic importance was real: possessing it implied a claim to imperial legitimacy, and it became a recurring plot device in both the novel and historical accounts. The coalition's disintegration is well documented historically. Yuan Shao and Yuan Shu's rivalry was genuine and rooted in their complex family dynamics (one was the legitimate heir, the other the son of a concubine). Cao Cao's pursuit of Dong Zhuo and subsequent defeat at Xingyang is historical, and it deepened his contempt for the coalition lords who refused to commit their forces.

## Episode 7: The Death of Sun Jian (孙坚之死)

Sun Jian sails his army along the Yangtze back toward his base in Jiangdong. His passage takes him through territory controlled by Liu Biao, the governor of Jing Province. Despite initial signals of safe passage, Liu Biao, at the urging of his advisor Cai Mao, sets an ambush along the river. Sun Jian's fleet is attacked, and in the onslaught of arrows, Sun Jian is mortally wounded.

In a tearful farewell on the blood-soaked deck, the dying Sun Jian curses Yuan Shao for his ingratitude and entrusts the Jade Seal to Sun Quan, the younger of his two sons present. He instructs Sun Quan to deliver it to Yuan Shu as leverage for a political alliance. Sun Jian's body falls into Liu Biao's hands, along with many of the Jiangdong warships. The nine-year-old Sun Quan, displaying the composure and cunning that will define his later career, negotiates with Liu Biao directly. He forges a letter in his father's name expressing forgiveness and a desire for reconciliation, which moves the guilt-ridden Liu Biao enough to return Sun Jian's body in exchange for the captured general Huang Zu.

In Chang'an, Li Ru reports Sun Jian's death to Dong Zhuo, who laughs with relief at the elimination of one of his most capable enemies. Meanwhile, Dong Zhuo executes the official Zhang Wen for secretly communicating with Yuan Shao, using his blood to toast the terrified court officials. Back in Wang Yun's residence, the old minister nearly kills himself in despair but is again stopped by Diao Chan. He learns that Diao Chan and Lu Bu have already developed feelings for each other during their earlier encounters, and a plan begins to crystallize in his mind.

**Historical significance:** Sun Jian's death in battle against Liu Biao's forces at Xiangyang is historical (191 AD). However, the circumstances differ: historically, Sun Jian was killed in a land battle at Mount Xian, struck by falling rocks or arrows depending on the source. The novel's version with the river ambush is more dramatic. Sun Jian's death left his sons Sun Ce and Sun Quan to build what would become the kingdom of Wu. The young Sun Quan's composure here foreshadows his later role as one of the three great rulers. Dong Zhuo's execution of Zhang Wen is historical and illustrates the reign of terror he maintained in Chang'an.

## Episode 8: The Beauty Stratagem Begins (美人连环计)

Wang Yun, his despair now giving way to calculation, receives Lu Bu at his residence for a lavish banquet. He showers the warrior with praise for his courage and loyalty, and Lu Bu, always susceptible to flattery, drinks it in. When Diao Chan appears to serve wine and play the pipa, Lu Bu is transfixed by her beauty. In a private moment, Lu Bu confesses his deep feelings for Diao Chan to Wang Yun and, in a gesture of filial devotion, kneels and asks to call Wang Yun his adoptive father. Wang Yun, overjoyed, formally promises Diao Chan to Lu Bu in marriage.

Days later, Wang Yun hosts a separate banquet for Dong Zhuo himself. Diao Chan, elaborately dressed, performs a dance that leaves the tyrant utterly mesmerized. Dong Zhuo, even more captivated than Lu Bu had been, declares that such a beauty should be presented to the Emperor as a consort. Wang Yun agrees with exaggerated deference. But that night, the imperial sedan does not go to the palace; instead, it travels directly to Dong Zhuo's private estate at Meiwu.

When Lu Bu discovers that the woman promised to him has been taken by his adoptive father, he confronts Wang Yun in a fury. Wang Yun, playing the innocent, explains that Dong Zhuo commandeered Diao Chan and that he was powerless to resist. He subtly stokes Lu Bu's rage while maintaining his own appearance of helplessness. Lu Bu, desperate, goes to Dong Zhuo the next morning and begs his lord to give Diao Chan to him instead, but Dong Zhuo flatly refuses. Lu Bu leaves humiliated and seething.

**Historical significance:** The "chain of beauty" stratagem (连环计) is one of the most famous schemes in the novel, though it has no historical basis. Diao Chan herself may be entirely fictional; no woman by that name appears in any historical record. The *Book of the Later Han* mentions only that Lu Bu had a secret affair with one of Dong Zhuo's serving women, and that fear of discovery contributed to his willingness to turn against Dong Zhuo. The novel transformed this brief mention into an elaborate scheme engineered by Wang Yun. In the 2010 series, the adaptation adds a genuine romantic dimension between Lu Bu and Diao Chan that was absent from the novel, where Diao Chan was more purely a tool of political manipulation.

---

## Episode 9: The Phoenix Pavilion (凤仪亭)

The tension between Dong Zhuo and Lu Bu escalates toward a breaking point. Lu Bu, unable to stay away, sneaks into Dong Zhuo's estate while the tyrant is attending a court session and finds Diao Chan in the gardens at the Phoenix Pavilion (凤仪亭). In a tearful reunion, Diao Chan tells Lu Bu that Dong Zhuo has violated her, and Lu Bu is overcome with grief and rage.

Dong Zhuo returns unexpectedly and discovers them embracing. Roaring with fury, the massive warlord hurls his halberd at Lu Bu, who barely dodges in time. A chase ensues through the gardens, but the obese Dong Zhuo cannot keep up with the agile Lu Bu, who escapes with his life. Lu Bu flees to Wang Yun's residence in a panic, confessing that Dong Zhuo means to kill him.

Wang Yun, concealing his satisfaction, counsels patience. He advises Lu Bu to formally seek Diao Chan's hand through proper channels rather than hide. After Lu Bu leaves, Wang Yun discovers that Diao Chan's tears were not entirely an act: she has genuinely fallen in love with Lu Bu. This complicates the scheme but does not derail it.

Wang Yun then orchestrates another feast for Dong Zhuo, at which Diao Chan once again performs, this time in even more dazzling attire. Dong Zhuo is so delighted that he promotes Wang Yun and showers him with honors. But the rift between Dong Zhuo and Lu Bu is now irreparable. Wang Yun's stratagem has succeeded in transforming the most powerful warlord-bodyguard relationship in the realm into a jealous rivalry over one woman.

**Historical significance:** The Phoenix Pavilion scene is among the most dramatized moments in the entire Three Kingdoms tradition, featured in opera, film, and art for centuries. Historically, we know only that Lu Bu feared Dong Zhuo's wrath after conducting an affair with one of his women, and that this fear was one factor in his later betrayal. The elaborate scheming by Wang Yun is the novel's invention. In real history, Wang Yun was indeed the chief conspirator behind Dong Zhuo's assassination, but he worked through political channels rather than romantic manipulation. The 2010 series deepens the emotional stakes by making Diao Chan's love for Lu Bu genuine, adding moral complexity to a character traditionally portrayed as a calculating seductress.

## Episode 10: The Death of Dong Zhuo (董卓之死)

Wang Yun returns home to find Chen Gong, his old friend and Cao Cao's former companion, waiting for him. Chen Gong has seen through the chain stratagem and names it openly, but rather than expose it, he offers his support. He argues that Lu Bu, the greatest warrior alive, can be turned into a weapon for righteous men if properly handled. As they confer, Lu Bu himself bursts in, frantic and afraid. Wang Yun and Chen Gong together persuade him that killing Dong Zhuo is not betrayal but duty: it is the act of a hero liberating the Emperor and the people. Lu Bu swears an oath to heaven that he will destroy the tyrant.

Wang Yun devises the final trap. He and the court officials pretend to endorse Dong Zhuo's ambition to declare himself Emperor, organizing an elaborate enthronement ceremony. On the appointed day, as Dong Zhuo arrives at the palace in full imperial regalia, Wang Yun gives the signal. Lu Bu steps forward and drives his halberd through Dong Zhuo's body. The tyrant dies in the street, and the common people of Chang'an celebrate in the streets, literally lighting candles in Dong Zhuo's navel fat, so hated was he.

The victory is short-lived. Wang Yun, intoxicated by success, ignores Chen Gong's counsel to offer amnesty to Dong Zhuo's remaining generals, Li Jue and Guo Si. Instead, he rebuffs their surrender. Desperate and with nothing to lose, Li Jue and Guo Si rally their Western Liang troops and attack the poorly defended capital. They capture the Emperor, and Wang Yun, refusing to flee, throws himself from the city wall in a final act of defiance.

In a parallel storyline, the episode introduces the first stirrings of Cao Cao's rise to power. Tao Qian, the governor of Xu Province, attempts to curry favor with Cao Cao by hosting his father Cao Song during a journey. But the escort troops, tempted by the Cao family's wealth, murder Cao Song on the road. This sets the stage for Cao Cao's vengeful invasion of Xu Province. Lu Bu and Diao Chan consummate their marriage at Meiwu, sleeping in the bed that once belonged to Dong Zhuo.

**Historical significance:** Dong Zhuo's assassination by Lu Bu in 192 AD is one of the most consequential events of the late Han period. The historical account largely matches the dramatic version: Wang Yun orchestrated a plot, and Lu Bu killed Dong Zhuo during what was supposed to be a ceremony. The people of Chang'an did celebrate, and historical sources confirm the gruesome detail about lighting Dong Zhuo's body fat as a candle. However, the aftermath played out as shown: Wang Yun's refusal to pardon Li Jue and Guo Si was a catastrophic political blunder that led to the sack of Chang'an and years of further chaos. Wang Yun's death (historical, 192 AD) marked the end of any hope for restoring central authority through the old court aristocracy. The murder of Cao Song (historically placed in 193 or 194 AD) was the trigger for Cao Cao's brutal campaign against Xu Province, one of the bloodiest episodes of the era. The series compresses these events to maintain narrative momentum.
