# New Three Kingdoms (新三国) - Episodes 11-20
*The contest for Xuzhou, the fall of Lu Bu, and the forging of Cao Cao's supremacy*

These episodes follow the collapse of the anti-Dong Zhuo coalition into a free-for-all among warlords. The center of gravity shifts to Xuzhou province, where Cao Cao, Liu Bei, and Lu Bu each compete for control. By the end of this arc, Lu Bu is dead, Cao Cao has enthroned himself as the true power behind the Han dynasty, and Liu Bei has received a secret imperial mandate to destroy him. The arc closes with one of the most famous scenes in all of Chinese literature: two rivals sharing wine and a conversation about who truly deserves to be called a hero.

---

## Episode 11: Tao Qian Offers Xuzhou Thrice (陶恭祖三让徐州城)

Word reaches Cao Cao that his father Cao Song has been murdered while traveling through Xuzhou province. Cao Song had been en route to join his son in Yan Province when Zhang Kai, a subordinate of Xuzhou's governor Tao Qian, killed him and his entire retinue for their wealth. Cao Cao is shattered by grief, but his chief advisor Xun Yu sees the opportunity clearly: heaven itself has handed Cao Cao the pretext to seize Xuzhou.

Cao Cao mobilizes his full army and marches on Xuzhou under the banner of filial revenge. The elderly Tao Qian, dressed in mourning whites and accompanied by his sons, meets Cao Cao outside the city walls to beg forgiveness, taking personal responsibility for the crime. Cao Cao is unmoved. He declares he will take the city within three days. Tao Qian realizes that Cao Cao's grief is real but the invasion is strategic: revenge is the excuse, but conquest is the goal.

Desperate, Tao Qian sends urgent appeals for help to Yuan Shao, Yuan Shu, and other regional lords. Every one of them responds with empty sympathy and polite excuses, content to sit back and watch Xuzhou fall. The episode establishes a pattern that will recur throughout the series: in the post-coalition world, no one lifts a finger unless the outcome directly benefits them.

**Historical significance:** Cao Song's murder and Cao Cao's punitive campaigns against Xuzhou (193 and 194 AD) are well documented in historical sources. The Records of the Three Kingdoms and other texts note that Cao Cao's forces carried out brutal massacres of civilians during the Xuzhou campaigns, a detail this adaptation significantly softens. Tao Qian's repeated offers to cede the province to Liu Bei are drawn from Chapter 11 of the novel, with the "thrice declining" formula following the classical Chinese literary convention of refusing a gift three times before accepting. Historically, Tao Qian did favor Liu Bei as a successor, but the ritualized three-fold offer is a novelistic embellishment.

## Episode 12: Lu Bu Seeks Refuge with Liu Bei (吕奉先战败投刘备)

Liu Bei, accompanied by Guan Yu and Zhang Fei, arrives at Xuzhou to aid Tao Qian against Cao Cao's siege. A young officer who will later be known as Zhao Yun (Zhao Zilong) appears among the relief force. Liu Bei sends a letter to Cao Cao requesting him to accept Tao Qian's apology and withdraw, warning that he will stand with Xuzhou to the death if refused. Cao Cao is furious and prepares a full assault on the city.

Before the attack can begin, an urgent dispatch arrives from Cao Cao's home base: Lu Bu, defeated and homeless after being expelled from Chang'an, has swept into Yan Province and captured virtually all of Cao Cao's territory. Faced with losing everything, Cao Cao has no choice but to accept Liu Bei's ceasefire and march his army back west. He plays it as a magnanimous gesture, but everyone understands the real reason.

With Cao Cao gone, the dying Tao Qian summons Liu Bei to his bedside and, for the third time, presses the seal of office upon him, imploring him to govern Xuzhou. Liu Bei tries to defer to Yuan Shao, Yuan Shu, or Liu Biao, but Tao Qian will not hear of it. Only when Liu Bei finally agrees to serve as governor does the old man close his eyes in peace. Just as Liu Bei and his brothers are processing this sudden responsibility, the defeated Lu Bu appears at the city gates with his battered army, begging for shelter. Against the furious objections of Guan Yu and Zhang Fei, Liu Bei opens the gates. At a welcoming banquet, Lu Bu proposes that he and Liu Bei become sworn brothers, prompting Zhang Fei to storm out and publicly list Lu Bu's betrayals and crimes. Humiliated, Lu Bu offers to leave. Liu Bei, trying to manage the situation, settles Lu Bu at the nearby garrison town of Xiaopei. Far away, Cao Cao learns that Liu Bei now governs Xuzhou and curses his rival's cunning. Xun Yu counsels patience: with two tigers sharing one mountain, they need only wait for the inevitable clash.

**Historical significance:** Lu Bu's seizure of Yan Province behind Cao Cao's back (194 AD) is fully historical and was one of the pivotal strategic upsets of the era. It forced Cao Cao into years of fighting to reclaim his home base before he could project power elsewhere. Liu Bei's assumption of the Xuzhou governorship is also historical, though the circumstances were likely less ceremonial than depicted. The introduction of Zhao Yun (Zhao Zilong) in this arc compresses the novel's timeline; historically, Zhao Yun served under Gongsun Zan before eventually joining Liu Bei somewhat later. Zhang Fei's public denunciation of Lu Bu is a novelistic invention that dramatizes the deep distrust the brothers felt toward the serial betrayer.

## Episode 13: Cao Cao Rescues the Emperor (曹孟德救驾令诸侯)

Two major developments unfold in parallel. In the west, Emperor Xian has escaped from the warlords Li Jue and Guo Si, who had seized power in Chang'an after Dong Zhuo's assassination. The young emperor and a small retinue of loyalists, including the minister Dong Cheng, are fleeing eastward toward the old capital of Luoyang, sending desperate edicts to every warlord in the realm begging them to come to his rescue.

Cao Cao, having recaptured much of Yan Province from Lu Bu, immediately recognizes this as the opportunity of a lifetime. He mobilizes his army and races toward Luoyang. Meanwhile, Yuan Shao also receives the emperor's edict. His advisor Xu You urges him to seize the emperor, arguing that whoever holds the Son of Heaven holds the mandate of the realm. But other advisors counter that rescuing a powerless puppet is more trouble than it is worth, and Yuan Shao, fatally indecisive, does nothing.

Cao Cao arrives at the ruined Luoyang to find the emperor and court starving and destitute. He presents them with food, supplies, and the protection of his army. The grateful emperor weeps. The next day, Cao Cao accompanies the emperor to review his impressive troops, then delicately proposes relocating the capital to Xuchang, deep within Cao Cao's own territory. The ministers exchange uneasy glances: they have escaped one tyrant only to fall into the hands of another. Once in Xuchang, Cao Cao wastes no time. Using the emperor's seal, he issues a cascade of imperial edicts that formally assign territories to every warlord in the realm, effectively controlling the political chessboard. Yuan Shao, who passed on his chance, is reduced to accepting his title through gritted teeth.

**Historical significance:** The relocation of the emperor to Xuchang in 196 AD is one of the most consequential events in the entire Three Kingdoms period. Cao Cao's strategy of "using the Son of Heaven to command the lords" (挟天子以令诸侯) gave him a decisive advantage that no rival could match: the ability to issue orders with the stamp of imperial legitimacy. This was Xun Yu's counsel in the historical record, and it transformed Cao Cao from a regional strongman into the de facto ruler of China. Yuan Shao's failure to act on Xu You's advice is regarded by historians as one of his greatest blunders. The episode dramatizes this turning point faithfully, though it compresses events that unfolded over several months into a single dramatic sequence.

## Episode 14: Lu Bu Seizes Xuzhou by Night (吕奉先趁夜袭徐郡)

With Cao Cao now holding the emperor, he issues an imperial decree ordering Liu Bei to march against Yuan Shu, who has declared himself emperor in the south. Liu Bei reluctantly complies, leaving Zhang Fei to guard Xuzhou in his absence. This is a grave error. Zhang Fei, tasked with maintaining discipline, instead holds a banquet for the city's officials and demands that everyone drink. When Cao Bao, a local officer and Lu Bu's father-in-law, declines, Zhang Fei has him beaten. The humiliated Cao Bao sends a secret message to Lu Bu, inviting him to take the city.

Lu Bu strikes that very night. While Zhang Fei lies in a drunken stupor, Lu Bu's forces pour through the gates that Cao Bao has opened. By the time Zhang Fei wakes and fights his way out, Xuzhou has fallen. Liu Bei's family is captured. When Liu Bei returns from the campaign to find his city lost, his family taken, and Zhang Fei distraught with shame, he faces the most painful moment of his career to that point. With nowhere else to turn, Liu Bei swallows his pride and requests that Lu Bu allow him to station his small force at Xiaopei, the very garrison he had generously given Lu Bu just episodes earlier. Lu Bu, now master of Xuzhou, magnanimously agrees, and supplies are sent from the city. The roles have been completely reversed.

**Historical significance:** Lu Bu's seizure of Xuzhou from Zhang Fei (196 AD) is historical. The novel and this adaptation attribute it to Zhang Fei's drunkenness and Cao Bao's treachery, which follows the traditional account. Cao Cao's order for Liu Bei to attack Yuan Shu was a classic example of the "drive the tiger to swallow the wolf" stratagem (驱虎吞狼), credited to Xun Yu. The goal was to weaken both Liu Bei and Yuan Shu while creating an opening for Lu Bu. It worked perfectly. Liu Bei's humiliating reversal from Xuzhou's governor to a guest in his own former garrison town is a pivotal moment in his arc, reinforcing the theme that his path to power would be uniquely long and agonizing.

## Episode 15: Lu Bu Shoots the Halberd at the Gate (吕奉先辕门射画戟)

Yuan Shu, furious at Liu Bei for the military campaign against him, dispatches his general Ji Ling with a large army to destroy Liu Bei at Xiaopei. Liu Bei, with only a small garrison, sends desperate appeals to Lu Bu for help. Lu Bu, who needs Liu Bei alive as a buffer between himself and Cao Cao, devises a characteristic solution that is neither alliance nor warfare but pure spectacle.

Lu Bu invites both Liu Bei and Ji Ling to a banquet at his camp, set up between the two armies. He plants his famous crescent halberd in the ground at a distance of 150 paces from the banquet tent and proposes a wager: if he can shoot an arrow through the small side-blade of the halberd, both sides must withdraw in peace. If he misses, they may fight as they please. With a single, precise shot, Lu Bu splits the mark. Ji Ling, awed and intimidated, has no choice but to retreat. The scene is pure Lu Bu: solving a political problem through physical bravado rather than strategy.

Meanwhile, in the south, Sun Ce presents the Imperial Jade Seal to Yuan Shu in exchange for the loan of his late father Sun Jian's veteran officers and troops. Yuan Shu, intoxicated by possessing the Seal, declares himself emperor, establishing the short-lived Zhong dynasty. On a hilltop near his father's grave, Sun Ce reunites with his sworn brother Zhou Yu, and the two young men embrace and pledge to build an empire together in the Jiangdong region.

**Historical significance:** The halberd-shooting at the gate (辕门射戟) is one of the rare instances where a scene from the novel closely matches the historical record. Lu Bu's biography in the Records of the Three Kingdoms describes this event in nearly identical terms, and it likely occurred around 196 AD. Yuan Shu's self-proclamation as emperor in 197 AD is fully historical and was universally condemned, giving Cao Cao the moral justification to organize a campaign against him. Sun Ce's exchange of the Jade Seal for troops is a novelistic invention, but his establishment of a power base in Jiangdong with Zhou Yu's partnership is historically grounded. Sun Ce's campaign to conquer the region south of the Yangtze (194 to 199 AD) laid the foundation for the eventual kingdom of Wu.

## Episode 16: Cao Cao Defeats Yuan Shu (曹孟德举兵败袁术)

With imperial legitimacy now at his disposal, Cao Cao sets his sights on the self-proclaimed emperor Yuan Shu, whose usurpation provides the perfect legal pretext for war. Cao Cao mobilizes 170,000 troops and marches on Yuan Shu's capital at Shouchun. Along the way, Xun Yu advises him to simultaneously drive a wedge between Lu Bu and his potential allies, preventing any unified resistance.

In Xuzhou, the shrewd advisor Chen Gong sees through Yuan Shu's attempt to forge an alliance with Lu Bu through a marriage pact and firmly rejects the proposal. Lu Bu, offered an imperial title by Cao Cao through the emperor's authority, happily accepts the formal appointment as Governor of Xuzhou and even orders Liu Bei to contribute troops to the campaign against Yuan Shu.

During the march, the army's grain supplies run critically low. Cao Cao's quartermaster Wang Hou reports that rations will last only days. Cao Cao secretly instructs him to distribute short measures to stretch the supply, but when the troops begin to grumble about being cheated, Cao Cao has Wang Hou executed for corruption and displays his head to restore morale. To maintain discipline when his horse tramples a farmer's crops (violating his own strict orders), Cao Cao cuts off a lock of his own hair as symbolic self-punishment. Both acts demonstrate Cao Cao's willingness to sacrifice others and theatrically sacrifice himself to maintain control.

The campaign succeeds. Cao Cao's forces overwhelm Shouchun, and the "Emperor of Zhong" flees into the wilderness with the remnants of his court. Liu Bei takes his leave from Cao Cao, who warns him ominously that Lu Bu will not tolerate him for long and that he is welcome to come to Xuchang if things go badly.

**Historical significance:** Cao Cao's destruction of Yuan Shu (197 AD) removed one of the weakest but most provocative players from the board. Yuan Shu's self-proclamation as emperor had made him a universal target. The "cutting hair as punishment" (割发代首) anecdote appears in the Cao Man Zhuan (an unofficial biography of Cao Cao) and was popularized by the novel. Historians debate whether it actually happened, but it perfectly illustrates the Cao Cao archetype: a leader who performs justice while always ensuring the real consequences fall on someone else. The execution of the grain officer to deflect blame is similarly characteristic.

## Episode 17: Lu Bu Loses Xuzhou to Cao Cao's Schemes (中曹计吕布夺徐州)

Inside Xuzhou, Lu Bu has settled into complacency. He spends his days feasting with flatterers while his only capable advisor, Chen Gong, is increasingly marginalized. The Chen father and son (Chen Gui and Chen Deng), local gentry who are secretly working for Cao Cao, shower Lu Bu with praise at every banquet, gradually displacing Chen Gong's influence.

One day, while hunting in the hills, Chen Gong intercepts a courier carrying secret correspondence between Cao Cao and Liu Bei. The letters reveal that Liu Bei has been coordinating with Cao Cao behind Lu Bu's back. Enraged, Lu Bu ignores Chen Gong's counsel to proceed cautiously and immediately marches on Xiaopei to destroy Liu Bei. He leaves the Chen father and son to guard Xuzhou, exactly the opening they have been waiting for.

Outside Xiaopei, Chen Gong's battle plan lures Liu Bei out of his fortifications and into an ambush. The three brothers are scattered in the chaos of the fighting, and Liu Bei's position seems hopeless. But Cao Cao's main army arrives on the scene with overwhelming force, revealing the true scope of the trap. Lu Bu realizes too late that the entire sequence of events has been orchestrated: the intercepted letters, his own rage, his departure from the city. When he turns back toward Xuzhou, he finds that the Chen father and son have barred the gates against him. Lu Bu has lost his city, his army is in disarray, and he has no choice but to retreat to the fortress of Xiapi for a last stand.

**Historical significance:** The fall of Xuzhou through internal subversion (198 to 199 AD) reflects the historical reality that Cao Cao's greatest weapon was often not his army but his intelligence network. Chen Gui and Chen Deng were indeed local officials who worked against Lu Bu from within, and their defection is recorded in historical sources. Chen Gong's interception of the letters and the subsequent ambush are novelistic embellishments, but the underlying dynamic is accurate: Lu Bu was undone less by military defeat than by the collapse of loyalty around him. His inability to listen to Chen Gong, his only truly competent strategist, is a recurring theme in both history and fiction.

## Episode 18: Lu Bu Perishes at White Gate Tower (白门楼奉先赴黄泉)

Cao Cao's army lays siege to Xiapi, the last stronghold of Lu Bu's crumbling domain. Lu Bu, trapped inside with Chen Gong and his remaining loyalists, oscillates between defiance and despair. Cao Cao rides to the base of the city wall alone and calls up to Lu Bu, urging him to surrender and join forces. For a moment, Lu Bu seems tempted. But Chen Gong, standing on the rampart beside him, draws his bow and fires at Cao Cao, ending any possibility of negotiation.

The siege drags on. Cao Cao orders the nearby rivers diverted to flood Xiapi's defenses. As the waters rise and morale collapses, Lu Bu's own officers Song Xian and Wei Xu turn against him. They bind him while he sleeps and open the gates. Lu Bu, the greatest warrior of his age, is brought before Cao Cao in chains.

In the famous scene at White Gate Tower (白门楼), Lu Bu begs for his life, offering to serve Cao Cao as his supreme general. Cao Cao, who genuinely admires Lu Bu's martial prowess, appears to waver. He turns to Liu Bei and asks his opinion. Liu Bei delivers the devastating reply: "Have you forgotten what happened to Ding Yuan and Dong Zhuo?" Both were Lu Bu's former lords, and both were murdered by him. The words seal Lu Bu's fate. In this adaptation, Lu Bu is executed by arrows on the tower, a dramatic departure from both the novel and history. Chen Gong is also brought before Cao Cao. Cao Cao, who has never forgotten the man who once freed him from prison, offers him a position. Chen Gong refuses with quiet dignity, saying he has chosen his master and will share his fate. He walks to his execution without looking back, and Cao Cao, visibly moved, orders that Chen Gong's mother and family be cared for.

Diao Chan, who has witnessed everything, washes Lu Bu's body in silence. With Lu Bu's death, the series closes out its first great character arc: the tragedy of a man with unmatched physical gifts and no capacity for trust, strategy, or self-awareness.

**Historical significance:** The siege and flooding of Xiapi and Lu Bu's execution (199 AD) are thoroughly documented in historical sources. The betrayal by Song Xian and Wei Xu, Lu Bu's begging for mercy, and Liu Bei's devastating reminder about Ding Yuan and Dong Zhuo all appear in the Records of the Three Kingdoms. Historically, Lu Bu was strangled and then beheaded, with his head sent to the capital for display; the series' depiction of death by arrows is a dramatic invention. Chen Gong's dignified refusal and execution are also historical, and Cao Cao did indeed provide for his family afterward. The emotional weight of the Chen Gong farewell recalls their first meeting in Episode 2, when Chen Gong abandoned everything to follow a man he admired. Their final encounter, as enemies, is one of the series' most poignant callbacks.

## Episode 19: Liu Bei Is Summoned to the Capital (刘皇叔奉旨誓灭曹)

With Xuzhou conquered, Cao Cao's advisors debate what to do with the spoils and the people. Xun Yu, concerned that Cao Cao will become distracted by the captive Diao Chan, engineers her death: he goads the warrior Xu Chu into killing her, presenting it to Cao Cao as a fait accompli. Cao Cao is angry but accepts the logic. This cold disposal of a woman who has been central to the story since Episode 1 is the series' sharpest departure from the novel, where Diao Chan simply vanishes from the narrative without explanation.

A more consequential debate concerns who should govern Xuzhou. When local gentry submit a "Petition of Ten Thousand Names" (万民书) endorsing Liu Bei, Cao Cao's suspicion of his rival hardens. He decides that rather than leave Liu Bei in Xuzhou, he will bring him to the capital. Cao Cao invites Liu Bei to accompany him to Xuchang to receive honors from the emperor. Liu Bei, unable to refuse, agrees.

In Xuchang, Liu Bei meets Emperor Xian. When the emperor consults the imperial genealogy and discovers that Liu Bei is a distant member of the Han ruling family, the two men embrace as uncle and nephew, weeping together. Liu Bei is elevated to Left General and Marquis of Yicheng. But the real drama comes three days later at an imperial hunt at Xutian. During the hunt, Cao Cao borrows the emperor's ancestral bow and personally shoots a magnificent deer. The assembled troops erupt in cries of "Ten Thousand Years!" (万岁), the acclamation reserved for the Son of Heaven alone. Cao Cao makes no move to correct them, calmly accepting the ovation while the emperor sits pale and silent. Guan Yu's hand moves toward his blade, but Liu Bei restrains him with a look. That night, Cao Cao privately reflects that the moment to claim a royal title has not yet arrived and that he must not repeat Yuan Shu's mistake. Word comes that the emperor has fallen ill after the hunt, and Liu Bei rushes to the palace.

**Historical significance:** The Xutian hunting incident (许田射猎) is from Chapter 20 of the novel and has no firm historical basis, though it dramatizes the real political reality: by 199 AD, Cao Cao's power so thoroughly eclipsed the emperor's that the distinction between them was becoming a polite fiction. The killing of Diao Chan by Xu Chu is a pure invention of this 2010 series, found in neither the novel nor any historical source. Diao Chan herself is a fictional character with no historical counterpart. The series uses her death to underscore Cao Cao's faction as coldly pragmatic and to close out the Dong Zhuo storyline decisively. The "Petition of Ten Thousand Names" supporting Liu Bei reflects the novel's theme that Liu Bei commanded genuine popular loyalty, in contrast to Cao Cao's authority through force.

## Episode 20: Cao Cao and Liu Bei Discuss Heroes Over Wine (曹孟德煮酒论英雄)

Deep within the palace, Emperor Xian pours out his anguish to Liu Bei, describing his years of captivity and humiliation since Dong Zhuo's seizure of power. The emperor kneels before his own kinsman and begs him to rally the heroes of the realm against Cao Cao. Liu Bei, overwhelmed, swears a solemn oath to destroy the traitor and restore the Han dynasty.

The emperor then summons his father-in-law Dong Cheng to a private audience and presents him with an ornate palace sash. Hidden inside the silk lining is a secret edict written in the emperor's own blood, commanding loyal subjects to assassinate Cao Cao. Dong Cheng nearly fails to discover it, but when he does, he immediately seeks out Liu Bei. Together they read the blood edict, and Liu Bei formally accepts the mission. The "Belt Edict Conspiracy" (衣带诏) now has its champion.

Knowing he is under constant surveillance, Liu Bei adopts an elaborate disguise: he plants a vegetable garden behind his residence and spends his days watering cabbages, presenting himself as a harmless farmer with no ambitions. Cao Cao, not entirely fooled, invites Liu Bei to his residence for a private conversation. In a pavilion set among green plum trees, with wine warming over a brazier, Cao Cao steers the talk to the question of who in the realm deserves to be called a true hero. Liu Bei names one warlord after another: Yuan Shao, Yuan Shu, Liu Biao, Sun Ce, Liu Zhang. Cao Cao dismisses each one with cutting precision. Then he leans forward and delivers the line that stops Liu Bei's heart: "The only heroes in the world today are you and I." At that exact moment, a clap of thunder shakes the sky. Liu Bei drops his chopsticks in shock. When Cao Cao looks at him sharply, Liu Bei recovers and laughs, saying: "The sage Confucius taught that a gentleman trembles at the sound of thunder. Even nature's fury can rattle a man." Cao Cao accepts the excuse, his suspicion not quite confirmed.

Shortly after, news arrives that Yuan Shao has destroyed Gongsun Zan in the north and that the fugitive Yuan Shu is trying to flee northward to join his cousin. Liu Bei seizes the opening, volunteering to lead a force to intercept Yuan Shu, which would conveniently take him out of Xuchang and out of Cao Cao's grasp. With the emperor's quiet encouragement, Cao Cao agrees and issues the order. Liu Bei rides out of the capital at top speed, and once beyond Cao Cao's reach, he breathes freely for the first time in months. His mission to rally resistance against Cao Cao has begun.

**Historical significance:** The Belt Edict (衣带诏) conspiracy of 199 to 200 AD is documented in both the Records of the Three Kingdoms and the Book of the Later Han, though historians debate its exact details. Dong Cheng's involvement is historical; he was later discovered and executed along with his co-conspirators. Whether Liu Bei was truly part of the plot before leaving Xuchang is uncertain. The "Discussing Heroes Over Green Plum Wine" (青梅煮酒论英雄) scene is from Chapter 21 of the novel and is one of the most celebrated passages in all of Chinese literature. The dropped chopsticks and the thunder excuse are novelistic inventions, but they capture a dynamic that was historically real: Cao Cao recognized Liu Bei as a genuine long-term threat, while Liu Bei understood that survival required concealing his ambitions until the moment was right. Liu Bei's departure from Xuchang marks the true beginning of his independent career as a rival power, setting the stage for the wars that will eventually produce the Three Kingdoms.
