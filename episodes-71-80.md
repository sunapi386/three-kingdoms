# New Three Kingdoms (新三国) - Episodes 71-80
*From the Fall of Guan Yu to the Eve of Yiling: Betrayal, Succession, and the War of Vengeance*

## Episode 71: The Trap Closes on Jingzhou

Sun Quan receives a letter from Cao Cao urging him to seize Jingzhou while Guan Yu is distracted. The Wu strategist Lu Xun proposes a diplomatic ploy: offer a marriage alliance between the Sun and Liu families to gain leverage over Jingzhou. Zhuge Jin travels to Jingzhou as matchmaker, but Guan Yu contemptuously rejects the proposal, declaring his daughter unfit to marry the "dog of Wu." This insult gives Sun Quan the pretext he needs.

Guan Yu, consumed by ambition, strips Jingzhou of its entire garrison to launch a massive assault on Fan Castle. His initial attack falters and he takes an arrow wound, but he devises a brilliant stratagem: flooding the Wei positions. The waters surge and Guan Yu wins a spectacular victory, capturing the Wei commander Yu Jin and killing the defiant Pang De. His arrow wound festers, however, and the legendary physician Hua Tuo arrives to perform the famous operation of scraping poison from the bone while Guan Yu calmly plays chess. The scene showcases Guan Yu's superhuman composure, but also his fatal flaw: he believes himself invincible.

Meanwhile, Lu Meng feigns illness, and Sun Quan appoints the young, seemingly harmless Lu Xun as acting Grand Commander. This is a ruse. Guan Yu, deceived into thinking Wu poses no threat, pulls his remaining Jingzhou reserves to the front. That night, Wu commandos seize the signal beacon towers along the river, and Lu Meng's main force advances unopposed into the heart of Jingzhou.

**Historical significance:** This episode compresses several chapters of the novel and several months of the historical Battle of Fancheng (219 CE) into a single dramatic arc. The flooding of Fan Castle (水淹七军) is one of Guan Yu's most celebrated victories and is attested in historical sources, though the novel dramatizes it considerably. Hua Tuo's bone-scraping surgery is almost certainly fictional; historians doubt Hua Tuo was alive at this point. Lu Meng's feigned illness and the "white-robed soldiers crossing the river" stratagem are historically documented and represent one of the most successful deception operations in Chinese military history. The loss of Jingzhou would reshape the entire strategic landscape of the Three Kingdoms, destroying Liu Bei's two-front capability and dooming Zhuge Liang's grand strategy.


## Episode 72: The Death of Guan Yu

The tide turns catastrophically against Guan Yu. Cao Cao dispatches Xu Huang with elite troops to relieve Fan Castle, and Xu Huang's forces smash through Guan Yu's eastern camp. Simultaneously, word arrives that Lu Meng has taken Jingzhou. Guan Yu's army, learning their families are now under Wu control and being treated well by Lu Meng, begins to disintegrate. Soldiers desert in waves, and Guan Yu's forces collapse like a mountain in a landslide.

Cao Cao, ever the strategist, orders his commanders to pursue Guan Yu but not to kill him, hoping to drive this crisis entirely onto Sun Quan's doorstep. Lu Meng, however, has no such restraint. He dispatches a crack force to hunt Guan Yu down. Guan Yu retreats to Mai Castle (麦城) with a pitiful remnant of his once-mighty army. Lu Xun urges restraint, advising against killing Guan Yu to avoid provoking Liu Bei into total war, but Lu Meng overrules him.

Cornered and surrounded, Guan Yu's last bodyguards fight to the death around him. When the final man falls, Guan Yu stands alone before the Wu soldiers. In the series' most significant departure from the novel, where Guan Yu is captured and executed, here he calmly strokes his beard, draws his blade, and takes his own life. It is a deliberate artistic choice by director Gao Xixi: the God of War will not be dragged before his enemies. Lu Meng severs the head and presents it as tribute to avenge Zhou Yu. Back in Wu, Lu Xun returns to find that Lu Meng has himself died shortly after, fulfilling the novel's theme that killing Guan Yu brings a curse upon its perpetrator.

**Historical significance:** Guan Yu's death in the winter of 219-220 CE is one of the most consequential events in Three Kingdoms history. In both the historical record and the novel, Guan Yu was captured by Wu forces under Ma Zhong and executed on Sun Quan's orders. The TV series changes this to a dignified suicide, reflecting the near-divine status Guan Yu holds in Chinese culture, where he was eventually deified as the God of War and Righteousness. Lu Meng's death shortly after is historical fact, though the novel attributes it to Guan Yu's vengeful ghost. The strategic consequence was enormous: Shu lost Jingzhou permanently, severing the northern and eastern prongs of the Longzhong Plan.


## Episode 73: The Death of Cao Cao

Lu Xun meets with Sun Quan and, having accomplished the Jingzhou campaign, requests to be relieved of the Grand Commander title and allowed to retire to study. This is characteristic Lu Xun: wielding power without clinging to it.

In Chengdu, the news of Guan Yu's death reaches Liu Bei, who collapses in grief. His anguish is total. In Xu Chang, Sun Quan sends Guan Yu's severed head to Cao Cao as a gift, attempting to redirect Liu Bei's rage northward. Cao Cao sees through the ploy instantly but nevertheless orders an honorable burial for Guan Yu, whom he has always respected as a warrior without equal. During the funeral, Cao Cao's chronic headaches flare with devastating intensity. His advisors urge him to summon Hua Tuo for treatment, but Cao Cao, paranoid to the end, refuses.

Sun Quan then urges Cao Cao to declare himself emperor. Cao Cao declines, recognizing it as a transparent attempt to make him a usurper and unite enemies against him. His condition deteriorates rapidly. On his deathbed, Cao Cao confronts his heir Cao Pi with a chilling question: did you murder your brother Cao Chong, the child prodigy I once intended as my successor? Cao Pi denies it. Whether satisfied or simply out of options, Cao Cao passes the mantle to Cao Pi and dies. With his death, an era ends. The man who unified northern China, who broke the old aristocratic order, who could quote poetry while planning massacres, is gone.

**Historical significance:** Cao Cao died on March 15, 220 CE, one of the most significant deaths in Chinese history. The scene where Sun Quan sends Guan Yu's head to Cao Cao is drawn from the novel, and the idea was indeed to frame Cao Cao for the killing. Cao Cao's headaches and his distrust of physicians are well-attested in historical sources, though the specific story about Hua Tuo varies between sources. The question about Cao Chong's murder is a dramatic invention; Cao Chong historically died of illness at age 12, though suspicions of foul play have circulated for centuries. Cao Cao's refusal to take the imperial title despite holding all real power is historical, and his famous line "if Heaven's mandate falls to me, let me be King Wen of Zhou" (letting his son be the one to formally establish a new dynasty) shows his political cunning even on his deathbed.


## Episode 74: The Seven Steps Poem

In the ancestral temple, the Han Emperor Xian weeps before the tablets of his forebears, terrified that the Han dynasty will end on his watch. His fears are well-founded.

Cao Zhang, commanding 100,000 troops, marches toward the capital ostensibly to mourn his father but perhaps to seize the throne. Cao Pi is alarmed. Sima Yi volunteers to handle the crisis and rides out to confront Cao Zhang directly. In a tense confrontation in Cao Zhang's military tent, Sima Yi browbeats the prince into surrendering his command and entering the capital alone to pay respects. It is Sima Yi's first major political triumph and a preview of the patience and ruthlessness that will eventually devour the Wei dynasty itself.

With that threat neutralized, Sima Yi advises Cao Pi to test Cao Zhi's loyalty by summoning him to mourn. Cao Pi rewards Sima Yi with the gift of a serving woman named Jingshu. When Cao Zhi arrives, Cao Pi accuses him of disrespect toward their late father and threatens execution. Their mother intervenes furiously, and Cao Pi promises no harm. But the next day in open court, he issues his challenge: compose a poem within seven steps, or die. Cao Zhi, tears streaming, recites the immortal Seven Steps Poem: "Boiling beans over a beanstalk fire / The beans weep within the pot / Born from the same root as we are / Why do you burn me with such haste?" Even Cao Pi is moved. Cao Zhi is spared but permanently sidelined.

**Historical significance:** The Seven Steps Poem (七步诗) is one of the most famous poems in the Chinese language, though its authenticity is debated. It first appears in the *Shishuo Xinyu* (5th century anecdote collection), not in the formal histories. The dramatic framing with the seven-step deadline comes from the 14th-century novel. Historically, Cao Zhi was indeed politically marginalized after Cao Pi's succession, moved between fiefdoms and kept under surveillance until his death in 232 CE. Sima Yi's role in resolving the Cao Zhang crisis is dramatized here but reflects his historical emergence as the indispensable advisor to the Cao family. The series wisely gives Sima Yi prominent screen time in these episodes, planting seeds for the endgame decades later.


## Episode 75: The Abdication

Sima Yi analyzes the political conditions and concludes the time is ripe for Cao Pi to formally replace the Han dynasty. Cao Pi agrees, but Sima Yi insists the initiative must appear to come from others. He arranges for the minister Hua Xin to lead a group of officials in demanding Emperor Xian's abdication in open court.

The Emperor, surrounded by hostile ministers and soldiers, visits the imperial temple in despair. Cao Hong and Cao Xiu arrive with armed troops to accelerate the process. Under this combined military and political pressure, Emperor Xian surrenders the imperial seal. The humiliation is total. Outside the palace, Cao Pi encounters his mother, Empress Dowager Bian, who bitterly rebukes him for destroying the dynasty their family served. Cao Pi shows no remorse.

In a scene that underlines the cruelty of the transition, Cao Pi rejects the first draft of the abdication edict, finding its language insufficiently submissive. He forces Emperor Xian to rewrite it, again and again, each draft more degrading than the last. That night, Sima Yi visits the broken emperor and reveals that Hua Xin has already begun constructing the abdication platform, the "Altar of the Yielded Mandate" (受禅台). The formality is a performance; the outcome was decided long ago.

**Historical significance:** The abdication of Emperor Xian on November 25, 220 CE, ended the Han dynasty after over four centuries. The historical process of "abdication by yielding" (禅让) was ostensibly voluntary and modeled on the ancient sage-kings Yao and Shun, but everyone understood it was coerced. This "polite usurpation" would become the standard template for dynastic transitions in Chinese history for centuries afterward. Hua Xin's role as the frontman pressuring the emperor is historically documented. The TV series dramatizes the repeated rewriting of the edict and adds the drowning of Emperor Xian (in the next episode), which is fictional; the historical Emperor Xian lived another 14 years as the Duke of Shanyang and died of natural causes in 234 CE.


## Episode 76: The End of Han and the Grief of Shu

Sima Yi personally drafts the final abdication edict while Cao Pi's consort grinds the ink. The next day, Cao Pi ascends the altar and officially proclaims the state of Wei, changing the era name and establishing his capital. Emperor Xian is stripped of his title and demoted to Duke of Shanyang, then expelled from the capital.

In the series' most dramatic fictional addition, the deposed emperor and his consort perish during their exile when their boat sinks in the river. This invented death scene transforms Emperor Xian from a historical footnote into a tragic figure, and it gives Sima Yi one final moment of dark agency: he is the one who arranges the journey.

In Chengdu, Liu Bei is devastated by a cascade of terrible news: Guan Yu is dead, Jingzhou is lost, and now the Han dynasty itself has been extinguished. The man who built his entire political identity on "restoring the Han" finds the object of his life's work simply erased. Officials urge him to proclaim himself emperor and carry on the Han legacy, but Liu Bei refuses, viewing it as presumptuous. He falls ill, and Zhuge Liang assumes management of all governmental affairs. In a shrewd political maneuver, Zhuge Liang then feigns his own illness, forcing Liu Bei to come visit him personally, where he renews the argument for Liu Bei to take the throne. Liu Bei begins consulting old ministers of Shu to build a broader base of support.

**Historical significance:** The fall of the Han dynasty is one of the defining moments of Chinese civilization. The Wei state that replaced it controlled all of northern China. Liu Bei's reluctance to declare himself emperor is well-documented and was likely genuine in part, though political theater also played a role: a ruler who appears to accept the throne reluctantly gains more legitimacy than one who seizes it eagerly. Zhuge Liang's role in persuading Liu Bei is attested in historical sources. The drowning of Emperor Xian is entirely fictional; in reality he lived comfortably until 234 CE, receiving respectful treatment under Wei rule.


## Episode 77: Liu Bei Becomes Emperor and Zhang Fei Falls

Liu Bei consults the veteran official Li Yan, who falls to his knees and urges Liu Bei to take the imperial title. Moved at last, Liu Bei agrees and appoints Li Yan to assist Zhuge Liang in governing. In the year 221 CE, Liu Bei formally declares himself Emperor of the Shu Han dynasty, claiming legitimate succession from the fallen Han. The coronation is both triumphant and haunted: the brotherhood that began in the Peach Garden is now missing two of its three members.

Immediately upon taking the throne, Liu Bei issues an edict to launch a punitive campaign against Eastern Wu to avenge Guan Yu. Zhao Yun steps forward to counsel restraint, arguing that the true enemy is Wei, not Wu. Zhuge Liang supports Zhao Yun, listing the strategic disadvantages of a war with Wu. Liu Bei reluctantly agrees to postpone.

But Zhang Fei, stationed in Langzhong, is incandescent with rage. He storms into Chengdu and confronts Liu Bei in his private chambers, demanding immediate war. Liu Bei calms him and sends him back to his garrison. Alone in Langzhong, Zhang Fei drowns his fury in alcohol, night after night. Drunk, he savagely beats and punishes his subordinates for the slightest infraction. The abuse escalates until two of his officers, Fan Jiang and Zhang Da, can endure no more. One night, while Zhang Fei lies in a drunken stupor, they creep into his tent and cut off his head. They flee with it to Eastern Wu.

**Historical significance:** Liu Bei declared himself emperor in Chengdu in April 221 CE, establishing the state of Shu Han. Zhang Fei's murder by his own officers Fan Jiang and Zhang Da is historical fact, recorded in the *Book of Shu* (蜀书) section of the *Sanguozhi* (Records of the Three Kingdoms). The historical sources confirm that Zhang Fei was known for his cruelty toward subordinates even as he was respectful toward scholar-officials, a character flaw that Liu Bei himself reportedly warned him about. The novel and series both use Zhang Fei's death to remove the last restraint on Liu Bei's obsession with vengeance, making the eastern campaign inevitable.


## Episode 78: Liu Bei's Eastern Campaign Begins

Before Zhang Fei's coffin, Liu Bei weeps with a grief that has hardened into pure hatred. He overrides all opposition and formally declares war on Eastern Wu, mobilizing an army of 700,000 (a figure inflated by the novel; the historical number was closer to 40,000-75,000). Zhuge Liang, unable to dissuade Liu Bei, quietly instructs the advisor Ma Liang to travel with the army and send regular reports on geography, troop dispositions, and battle conditions back to Chengdu.

Wei Yan and Ma Su visit Zhuge Liang, and Wei Yan provocatively suggests that Liu Bei intends to marginalize the Prime Minister. Zhuge Liang angrily dismisses him. The scene reveals the fractures in Shu's internal politics even at the moment of its greatest military commitment.

Sun Quan, receiving intelligence of the invasion, orders Sun Huan to hold the city of Zigui as a forward defense. The Shu army sweeps forward and takes Zigui in its first assault, a decisive opening victory. Defeat notices pile up in the Wu capital. In desperation, the Dowager Empress proposes returning Lady Sun (Sun Shangxiang, Liu Bei's estranged wife) to Shu to extinguish Liu Bei's anger. Sun Quan sends Zhuge Jin with a peace offer: return three commanderies of Jingzhou and Lady Sun. Liu Bei rejects it entirely, demanding that Sun Quan surrender with all his officials. In the Wu palace, Lady Sun, learning that full-scale war between her birth family and her husband's kingdom is now inevitable, is overcome with grief.

**Historical significance:** Liu Bei's eastern campaign, launched in July 221 CE, is known historically as the Battle of Xiaoting or the Battle of Yiling. The 700,000 troop figure is a classic novelistic exaggeration; modern historians estimate the actual Shu force at 40,000 to 75,000 and the Wu defense at roughly 50,000. The peace overture involving Lady Sun is a fictional addition. Historically, Liu Bei's decision to attack Wu instead of Wei was considered a strategic blunder by later analysts, including Zhuge Liang, who is said to have lamented: "If Fa Zheng were still alive, he could have persuaded the Emperor not to go east." Ma Liang's presence with the army is historical and would prove significant in the aftermath of the campaign.


## Episode 79: Sun Quan Bows to Wei, and Huang Zhong's Last Battle

With Liu Bei's forces bearing down, Zhuge Jin returns to Wu and counsels Sun Quan to submit to Cao Pi in order to secure his northern border. The veteran statesman Zhang Zhao supports this humiliating but pragmatic strategy, recommending the envoy Zhao Zi for the mission. In the Wei court, Cao Pi subjects Zhao Zi to a series of insulting questions, but the envoy responds with composure and dignity. Impressed despite himself, Cao Pi accepts Wu's submission and grants Sun Quan the title of King of Wu. Sima Yi advises Cao Pi to stand back and let the two southern powers exhaust each other.

Sun Huan, retreating from Zigui, regroups at Yiling, the last natural chokepoint before the Wu heartland. Liu Bei decides to besiege the city while destroying any relief forces, hoping to annihilate the Wu army in detail. Sun Quan commits a relief force of 100,000 troops. As these reinforcements depart, Lu Xun loudly protests the decision, seeing the danger. In the ensuing battle, the old Shu general Huang Zhong volunteers to serve as bait, luring the Wu reinforcements into a Shu ambush. Huang Zhong takes multiple arrow wounds during the operation but succeeds in drawing the enemy into the trap. It is a selfless act by a warrior who has nothing left to prove.

**Historical significance:** Sun Quan's submission to Cao Pi is well-documented and took place in 221 CE. It was a purely tactical move: Sun Quan accepted the title "King of Wu" from Wei to prevent a two-front war, with no intention of genuine subordination. He would eventually declare his own independent empire in 229 CE. Sima Yi's advice to exploit the Shu-Wu conflict reflects the historical Wei strategy of letting its rivals weaken each other. Huang Zhong's death in battle during Liu Bei's eastern campaign is a novelistic invention; the historical Huang Zhong died of illness in 220 CE, before the campaign began. The novel places him here for dramatic resonance, giving the last of Liu Bei's "Five Tiger Generals" (besides Zhao Yun and Ma Chao) a warrior's death.


## Episode 80: Lu Xun Takes Command

The aftermath of the battle at Fuchi is devastating for Wu. The veteran general Cheng Pu dies from arrow wounds, and Huang Zhong succumbs to his injuries shortly after. Liu Bei, grief-stricken but steeled by rage, vows to press forward until Sun Quan himself is destroyed. The Shu army advances toward Yiling, the last barrier before the Wu heartland.

Sun Quan, recognizing that his conventional commanders are outmatched, makes a bold decision: he appoints the young scholar-general Lu Xun as supreme commander of all Wu forces. It is a controversial choice. Lu Xun is young, bookish, and untested in independent command. But Sun Quan sees in him something his other generals lack: the strategic patience to fight a defensive war of attrition against an enemy driven by emotion.

Lu Xun immediately orders a general retreat and consolidation, abandoning forward positions to concentrate forces at Xiaoting. His commanders are furious. Officials petition Sun Quan to replace Lu Xun, accusing him of cowardice. Sun Quan refuses. Lu Xun's strategy becomes clear as Wu forces dig in at roughly fifty fortified positions along the river corridor. When Liu Bei launches assault after assault, the Wu defenders hold firm, inflicting heavy casualties. The Shu momentum stalls. Liu Bei, who expected a quick, cathartic war of vengeance, finds himself bogged down in exactly the kind of grinding campaign that favors the defender. The seeds of his catastrophic defeat at Yiling are being sown.

Meanwhile, in the Wu court, Fan Jiang and Zhang Da arrive with Zhang Fei's severed head, describing how they killed him. Sun Quan examines the head and declares, admiringly, "What a fierce Zhang Fei!" He then orders the two assassins seized, recognizing that men who murder their own lord are useful to no one and trusted by no one.

**Historical significance:** Lu Xun's appointment as commander is one of the great turning points of the Three Kingdoms era. Historically, Lu Xun (陆逊, 183-245 CE) was indeed seen as too young and scholarly by the veteran Wu generals, who resisted his authority. His strategy of strategic retreat and patient defense is well-attested and represents a textbook case of trading space for time against a numerically superior but overextended enemy. The death of Cheng Pu at Yiling is fictional; the historical Cheng Pu died years earlier, around 210 CE. Sun Quan's handling of Fan Jiang and Zhang Da varies between sources; in some accounts he sends them back to Shu in chains as a peace gesture, while in others he executes them. The stage is now set for the Battle of Xiaoting (夷陵之战), where Lu Xun's fire attack will destroy Liu Bei's army and effectively end Shu's ability to project power beyond its mountain borders.
