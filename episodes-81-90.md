# New Three Kingdoms (新三国) - Episodes 81-90
*From Yiling's Flames to the Northern Expeditions: The Passing of the Old Guard*

These ten episodes span some of the most consequential transitions in the Three Kingdoms saga. Liu Bei's disastrous campaign against Wu ends in fire and death at Baidicheng. The founding generation passes from the stage as Cao Pi, Liu Bei, and eventually Zhao Yun all die within a few years of each other. Power transfers to a new generation: Liu Shan inherits Shu, Cao Rui inherits Wei, and Sima Yi begins his slow, patient accumulation of military authority. Zhuge Liang, the last titan standing from the old era, pours everything into his Northern Expeditions against Wei, producing some of the most famous episodes in Chinese literary history: the Memorial on Dispatching the Troops, the loss of Jieting, the Empty Fort Strategy, and the tearful execution of Ma Su.

Notable omission: The 2010 series entirely cuts Zhuge Liang's southern campaign against Meng Huo and the famous "Seven Captures, Seven Releases" (七擒七纵). The adaptation jumps directly from the consolidation of Shu after Liu Bei's death to the preparations for the Northern Expeditions. This is one of the most discussed editorial choices of the series.

## Episode 81: Lu Xun Takes Command

Liu Bei's massive expeditionary force stretches across hundreds of li in linked encampments along the Yangtze. Advisor Ma Liang urges caution, pointing out that Lu Xun's repeated retreats are not cowardice but a deliberate strategy to wait for the right moment to strike. Liu Bei dismisses these concerns with contempt, boasting that he will grind Sun Quan into paste. He forbids Ma Liang from sending camp diagrams back to Zhuge Liang in Chengdu, seeing it as an insult to his own military judgment.

In Xu Chang, Sima Yi analyzes the situation for Cao Pi. He observes that Liu Bei's linked camps stretching four hundred li constitute a cardinal sin of military strategy, and predicts that if Lu Xun uses fire, the Shu army will be annihilated in a single battle. Sima Yi advises Cao Pi to prepare two forces: one to cut off Lu Xun's retreat and another to strike at Wu's undefended capital.

Meanwhile, in a secluded temple, Lu Xun has an audience with Sun Quan that will change the course of the war. Lu Xun lays out his three-part strategy: avoid the enemy's sharp edge, seize the initiative through patience, and destroy them when the moment comes. He declares that Liu Bei's eighty-thousand-strong army (inflated to hundreds of thousands in the drama's telling) is doomed because Liu Bei has become the very thing that guarantees defeat: a commander blinded by arrogance. Sun Quan, deeply impressed, orders an altar built overnight and formally appoints Lu Xun as Grand Commander of all Wu forces.

**Historical significance:** This episode sets the stage for the Battle of Yiling (夷陵之战, 222 CE), one of the three decisive battles of the Three Kingdoms period alongside Guandu and Red Cliffs. The historical Liu Bei's force was likely in the range of 40,000 to 50,000 troops, not the "800,000" of the novel's dramatic inflation. Lu Xun's appointment as overall commander at the young age of 39, over the heads of more senior Wu generals, was historically accurate and reflected Sun Quan's remarkable judgment of talent. Sima Yi's shrewd analysis of the linked camps, while dramatically embellished, captures the real strategic calculus that Wei was closely watching this conflict for opportunities to exploit.

## Episode 82: Fire Consumes the Linked Camps

Lu Xun finally launches the attack he has been planning for months. Under cover of darkness, Wu soldiers carrying bundles of dry straw infiltrate the gaps between Liu Bei's sprawling camps. The southeastern wind fans the flames through seven hundred li of connected encampments. The screen fills with fire and chaos as Liu Bei's army, caught in the height of summer heat among forests and wooden fortifications, is consumed. Liu Bei barely escapes with his life as his generals sacrifice themselves to buy him time.

In Chengdu, Zhuge Liang has anticipated this disaster. He has already ordered a stone formation (the Eight Trigram Array) placed along the river to slow any Wu pursuit, and dispatches Zhao Yun with a relief force to escort Liu Bei to safety at Baidicheng (White Emperor City). The stone maze confuses and delays Wu forces long enough for Liu Bei to reach the fortress.

Sun Quan, wary of Cao Pi exploiting Wu's exposed northern border, sends Zhuge Jin (Zhuge Liang's brother) to Baidicheng with a remarkable gesture: he returns all captured Shu grain and military equipment and proposes restoring the Sun-Liu alliance. Liu Bei, humiliated and physically broken, coughs blood at the sight of the envoy. But the next day, with what remains of his strategic mind still functioning, he agrees to the renewed alliance. He begins making arrangements for what he now knows is coming: his own death. He summons his son Liu Shan, his minister Li Yan, and begins the process of entrusting the future of Shu-Han to others.

**Historical significance:** The Battle of Yiling (also called the Battle of Xiaoting) in the summer of 222 CE was a catastrophic defeat for Shu. The fire attack is historical, as is Liu Bei's retreat to Baidicheng. The stone formation (八阵图) that supposedly blocked Lu Xun is a novel invention, though stone arrangements attributed to Zhuge Liang did exist as landmarks. The return of captured supplies and the peace overture from Wu are based on real events: Sun Quan genuinely feared a Wei attack from the north and needed to stabilize the Shu border quickly. Liu Bei never left Baidicheng after this retreat. He remained there, declining in health, until his death the following spring.

## Episode 83: The Entrustment at Baidicheng

In the spring of 223 CE, Liu Bei lies dying at Baidicheng. He summons Zhuge Liang from Chengdu for a final audience. Their conversation is one of the most celebrated scenes in Three Kingdoms literature. Liu Bei entrusts the fate of Shu-Han and his son Liu Shan to Zhuge Liang, speaking the famous and deeply ambiguous words: "If you judge that my son can be assisted, then assist him. If he proves unworthy, you may take the throne yourself." Zhuge Liang weeps and swears that he will exhaust his loyalty and his life in service to the Han restoration, never wavering until death. Liu Bei also charges Li Yan as co-regent and makes final arrangements for his other sons. He then dies, bringing to an end the story of the man who rose from a straw-sandal weaver to Emperor of Shu-Han.

The news of Liu Bei's death ripples outward. Sun Quan is relieved, calculating that Shu will now focus entirely on defending against Wei rather than seeking revenge against Wu. In Xu Chang, Cao Pi is delighted and immediately begins planning to exploit the power vacuum. Sima Yi devises a five-pronged strategy to attack Shu from multiple directions, hoping to overwhelm the young and untested regime before Zhuge Liang can stabilize it. Cao Pi enthusiastically approves the plan but once again denies Sima Yi's request for direct command of the armies, keeping him at arm's length from real military power.

Wei launches four armies against Shu. The young emperor Liu Shan panics and rushes to the chancellor's residence to beg Zhuge Liang for help. Zhuge Liang, with remarkable composure, reveals that he has already neutralized every threat. His calm mastery of the crisis demonstrates that the real power in Shu now resides entirely with the chancellor. However, the Wu alliance remains uncertain, and Zhuge Liang dispatches Ma Su as envoy to Jianye to shore it up.

**Historical significance:** The "Entrustment at Baidicheng" (白帝城托孤) of 223 CE is one of the most analyzed events in Chinese history. Liu Bei's offer to let Zhuge Liang take the throne has been debated for nearly two millennia: was it genuine, or was it a calculated test of loyalty? Historians are divided, but the consensus leans toward it being a combination of genuine trust and political theater designed to bind Zhuge Liang morally before the assembled court. The co-regency with Li Yan was historical, as was the immediate Wei offensive exploiting Liu Bei's death. Sima Yi's strategic plan is dramatized, but the historical record confirms that Wei did probe Shu's defenses aggressively during this transition period. In reality, Zhuge Liang managed the crisis masterfully, as shown in the episode.

## Episode 84: The Death of Cao Pi and the Memorial on Dispatching the Troops

Ma Su travels to Wu and, through sharp diplomacy, persuades Sun Quan to maintain the Shu-Wu alliance against Wei. With the border secured, Shu's forces repel the Wei invasion on all fronts.

In Xu Chang, Cao Pi ignores Sima Yi's counsel and personally leads a force of 350,000 against Wu. The campaign unfolds exactly as Sima Yi predicted: the combined resistance of Shu and Wu crushes the Wei offensive, and Cao Pi nearly loses his life in the debacle. Chastened by this humiliation, Cao Pi finally does what he has resisted for years. He grants Sima Yi real military authority and charges him with guiding his son Cao Rui. Shortly after, Cao Pi's chronic illness flares and he dies.

Young Cao Rui ascends the Wei throne. The Cao clan loyalists, led by Cao Zhen and Cao Xiu, immediately urge the new emperor to neutralize the threat of Sima Yi. Cao Rui compromises: he banishes Sima Yi to a frontier garrison in the Yong-Liang region, far from the capital. But Sima Yi, rather than despairing, recognizes the opportunity. Guarding the western frontier means building an independent military base and cultivating personal loyalty among the troops. The seeds of the Sima family's eventual rise to supreme power are planted in this exile.

In Chengdu, the episode closes with Zhuge Liang composing the Memorial on Dispatching the Troops (出师表, Chu Shi Biao), one of the most famous documents in Chinese literature. In it, he lays out the case for the Northern Expeditions against Wei, invokes the memory of Liu Bei, and commits himself to the restoration of the Han dynasty or death in the attempt.

**Historical significance:** Cao Pi died in 226 CE at the age of 39. His decision to entrust Sima Yi with military power, paralleling Liu Bei's entrustment of Zhuge Liang, set the stage for the eventual Sima takeover of Wei decades later. Cao Rui (Emperor Ming of Wei) was actually a capable ruler who managed the Sima threat skillfully for his lifetime. The Memorial on Dispatching the Troops (227 CE) is one of the most celebrated pieces of Chinese prose. The saying "Reading the Chu Shi Biao without tears means one is not a loyal subject" (读出师表不下泪者，其人必不忠) has been repeated for centuries. The memorial is historically authentic. Sima Yi's "exile" to the frontier is dramatized but based on the real tension between the Sima and Cao factions at court.

## Episode 85: Zhuge Liang Prepares for the Northern Campaigns

With the Memorial on Dispatching the Troops submitted to Emperor Liu Shan, Zhuge Liang begins assembling the Northern Expedition force. Before departing, he strips Li Yan of his personal troops and incorporates them into the expeditionary army, consolidating all military power under his direct control. He also receives intelligence that Meng Da, a former Shu general who had defected to Wei, is prepared to switch sides again.

On the Wei side, the young Emperor Cao Rui, despite the protests of the elder statesman Wang Lang, appoints the son of the late Xiahou Dun as Grand General with 200,000 troops to oppose the Shu invasion. Zhuge Liang's first strategic move is a masterful information operation: he spreads rumors designed to sow distrust between Sima Yi and the Wei court. Cao Rui takes the bait and personally travels to Yongliang to confront Sima Yi. Sima Yi, accompanied only by his sons, surrenders himself to Cao Rui and pleads for mercy. Cao Rui, moved by the old minister's apparent loyalty, spares his life but strips him of all military authority and sends him into retirement.

With Sima Yi neutralized, the Shu army surges forward, winning battle after battle against leaderless Wei forces. The climax of the episode is the confrontation between Zhuge Liang and Wang Lang before the assembled armies. Wang Lang rides out to persuade Zhuge Liang to surrender, delivering a lengthy speech about the mandate of heaven having passed to Wei. Zhuge Liang responds with a devastating verbal attack that systematically demolishes Wang Lang's arguments, his character, and the legitimacy of the Wei dynasty. The old minister, overwhelmed by rage and humiliation, falls from his horse and dies on the spot. Wei morale collapses. Cao Zhen attempts to exploit Wang Lang's death as a trap, but Zhuge Liang sees through the ruse and turns it into another Shu victory.

The episode also reveals that Zhuge Liang has successfully engineered Meng Da's defection back to Shu, setting up a potential second front deep inside Wei territory.

**Historical significance:** This episode compresses and dramatizes the opening moves of Zhuge Liang's first Northern Expedition (228 CE). The "scolding Wang Lang to death" (骂死王朗) is entirely fictional: the historical Wang Lang died of natural causes in 228, never confronted Zhuge Liang on any battlefield, and served as a distinguished minister. This scene is one of the novel's most famous inventions, showcasing Zhuge Liang's eloquence as a weapon. The Meng Da subplot is based on real events; Meng Da did secretly communicate with Zhuge Liang about defecting, but Sima Yi discovered the plot and acted with stunning speed to crush it. Notably, the 2010 series entirely omits the southern campaign against Meng Huo (七擒孟获), which occupied four full chapters in the novel and represents one of the most beloved storylines in the original text. The adaptation jumps directly from the consolidation of Shu to the Northern Expeditions.

## Episode 86: The Loss of Jieting and the Empty Fort

Sima Yi, despite his political disgrace, receives urgent intelligence: Meng Da has betrayed Wei and plans to seize the city of Xincheng as a staging ground for Shu. An old acquaintance named Shen Yi tips him off. Sima Yi acts without waiting for imperial authorization, riding through the night with an elite cavalry force. He reaches Xincheng before Meng Da's preparations are complete, storms the city, and executes the traitor. Sima Yi presents Meng Da's head to Emperor Cao Rui, who is so impressed by this display of initiative and loyalty that he restores Sima Yi to full military command, granting him authority over the armies defending both capitals.

Sima Yi immediately identifies the critical vulnerability in Zhuge Liang's campaign: the supply road through Jieting (Street Pavilion). Whoever holds Jieting controls whether the Shu army eats or starves. Ma Su, Zhuge Liang's protege, volunteers to defend this crucial chokepoint and signs a military oath guaranteeing success. When Ma Su arrives at Jieting, however, he ignores his deputy Wang Ping's protests and stations the entire force on top of a hill rather than guarding the road below. Sima Yi is overjoyed when he learns of this blunder. He surrounds the hill, cuts off the water supply, and shatters the Shu garrison. Wang Ping's small force fights bravely but cannot prevent the disaster.

Zhuge Liang, receiving Ma Su's deployment map, immediately recognizes that Jieting is lost and issues a cascade of emergency orders. But the damage is done: without Jieting, the entire Northern Expedition is unsustainable. Zhuge Liang personally travels to the small city of Xicheng to oversee the evacuation of supplies. When Sima Yi's main force unexpectedly appears outside the virtually undefended city, Zhuge Liang makes a desperate gamble. He orders the city gates thrown open, stations a few soldiers disguised as street sweepers outside, ascends the city wall, and begins playing the qin (zither). Sima Yi, suspecting an elaborate ambush from the man he considers the most dangerous strategist alive, orders a full retreat. The Empty Fort Strategy buys Zhuge Liang just enough time to complete the evacuation.

**Historical significance:** The loss of Jieting (228 CE) was a genuine and pivotal disaster for Shu. Ma Su's decision to camp on the hilltop against orders is confirmed in historical sources, and his execution afterward is also historical. However, the Empty Fort Strategy (空城计) is almost certainly fictional. It does not appear in the official histories (Chen Shou's Records of the Three Kingdoms) and was added by the novel. The historical Sima Yi was not even at Jieting; he was dealing with Meng Da at the time. The show combines two separate events (Sima Yi crushing Meng Da and the Jieting campaign) into a single, streamlined narrative. Despite being fictional, the Empty Fort Strategy has become one of the most famous military anecdotes in Chinese culture and one of the Thirty-Six Stratagems.

## Episode 87: Tears for Ma Su

The aftermath of the Jieting disaster unfolds. Zhuge Liang must decide what to do with Ma Su, the man whose arrogance cost Shu its best chance at conquering Wei. Ma Su is brought before the chancellor in chains. The scene that follows is one of the most emotionally wrenching in the entire series. Zhuge Liang, weeping openly, orders Ma Su's execution. He reminds the assembled officers that the late Emperor Liu Bei had explicitly warned him that Ma Su talks bigger than he can deliver and should never be given major responsibility. By trusting Ma Su despite this warning, Zhuge Liang acknowledges that the true fault lies with himself.

Ma Su's head is displayed before the army for three days as a warning, then sewn back onto his body and given a proper burial with full honors. Zhuge Liang composes a memorial of self-reproach to Emperor Liu Shan, writing: "Your servant is a man of mediocre talent, unable to enforce discipline and the laws, resulting in the loss of Jieting." He requests that the emperor strip him of his rank. Liu Shan, uncertain how to respond, settles on a compromise: Zhuge Liang is demoted to Right General but retains all the actual powers of the chancellorship and supreme military command.

A rare bright spot emerges from the retreat. Zhao Yun and Deng Zhi return with their forces completely intact, having conducted a textbook withdrawal under pressure. Zhuge Liang is astonished and declares: "Zhao Zilong is truly a great general." He offers Zhao Yun a reward of surplus silk, but the old warrior refuses, saying the army failed and there is nothing to celebrate. This moment highlights the character of the last surviving Tiger General, now elderly but still embodying the values of duty and humility.

**Historical significance:** The execution of Ma Su is one of the few events in this stretch that is solidly historical. The self-demotion is also documented: Zhuge Liang voluntarily reduced his own rank by three grades after the failure, an extraordinary act of accountability that cemented his reputation for integrity. Zhao Yun's orderly retreat is also confirmed in the historical record (though the details are sparse). The real Zhao Yun was indeed praised for his disciplined withdrawal, and he did refuse rewards. Zhuge Liang's self-reproach memorial exists in historical texts and was not a novelistic invention.

## Episode 88: The Death of Zhao Yun

The episode opens with one of the most poignant farewells in the series. Zhao Yun, the last of the Five Tiger Generals, falls ill and dies. In the drama's telling, his final words are a repeated cry of "Northern Expedition! Northern Expedition! Northern Expedition!" before he passes. Zhuge Liang, receiving the news, breaks down completely. "All my Tiger Generals are gone," he grieves. Zhao Yun's death severs the last living link to the brotherhood of Liu Bei, Guan Yu, and Zhang Fei that founded Shu-Han. From this point forward, Zhuge Liang carries the mission alone.

Steeling himself through grief, Zhuge Liang launches the second Northern Expedition. The target is Chencang, a heavily fortified Wei stronghold held by the determined garrison commander Hao Zhao. The siege proves costly and frustrating as Hao Zhao's defenses hold firm against repeated Shu assaults. Zhuge Liang shifts strategy, using Jiang Wei (a talented young Wei defector who has become his most promising protege) to lure Cao Zhen into a trap through a feigned defection. Cao Zhen falls for the deception and leads his forces into an ambush. Wei suffers significant losses.

However, the fundamental problem of the Northern Expeditions reasserts itself: supply lines. Shu's armies must cross mountain passes on narrow roads, and they simply cannot sustain a long campaign. With grain running low and no prospect of a quick victory at Chencang, Zhuge Liang orders a withdrawal southward. Cao Zhen, learning of the Shu retreat, eagerly pursues. During the withdrawal, Wei Yan turns and counterattacks, killing the Wei general Wang Shuang and briefly recapturing Chencang. The garrison commander Hao Zhao, seeing the situation is hopeless, takes his own life. Despite these tactical successes, the strategic reality remains: the second Northern Expedition has failed to achieve its objectives.

**Historical significance:** Zhao Yun died in 229 CE. The deathbed cry of "Northern Expedition!" is a dramatic invention, but his death during this period is historical. His passing symbolized the end of an era. The siege of Chencang (228-229 CE) is based on real events: Hao Zhao's defense of Chencang against Zhuge Liang's forces is recorded in the histories and was considered a brilliant example of defensive warfare. However, the historical Hao Zhao died of illness after the siege, not by suicide. Wang Shuang's death is historical. Jiang Wei's defection to Shu is also historical (228 CE) and Zhuge Liang did indeed see tremendous potential in the young officer, eventually grooming him as his military successor. The chronic supply problem that plagued every Northern Expedition was the central strategic reality that Zhuge Liang could never overcome.

## Episode 89: Zhuge Liang Defeats Sima Yi

Following the failure at Chencang, the rivalry between Zhuge Liang and Sima Yi intensifies into a direct confrontation. Sima Yi, now firmly in command of Wei's western defenses, imposes strict military discipline and adopts a strategy of patient defense: he will not be drawn into battle on Zhuge Liang's terms. When Zhuge Liang orders Wei Yan to attack the cities of Wudu and Yinping, attempting to force Sima Yi to dispatch relief forces into an ambush, Sima Yi sees through the gambit and sends generals Sun Li and Guo Huai on a limited response rather than committing his main force.

Wei Yan defeats the Wei relief column, but Zhuge Liang recognizes that Sima Yi's strategic patience represents a far more dangerous opponent than the reckless Cao Zhen. Each man takes the measure of the other across the mountains, and both conclude they are facing their true equal.

Zhuge Liang devises an audacious plan: he will use himself as bait, advancing into exposed territory at Wudu to tempt Sima Yi into a decisive engagement. Sima Yi, detecting the opportunity, dispatches the veteran general Zhang He to strike at the weakened Shu main camp while Zhuge Liang is away. But Zhuge Liang has anticipated exactly this move. Zhang He's forces march directly into a prepared killing ground. Surrounded by Shu troops, Zhang He fights with desperate valor but is overwhelmed. The great Wei general falls in battle, struck down by massed arrow fire in a narrow valley. His death is a severe blow to Wei's military capability.

**Historical significance:** The events depicted here draw primarily from the third Northern Expedition (229 CE) and portions of the fourth (231 CE), compressed for dramatic purposes. The capture of Wudu and Yinping commanderies was a real achievement of Zhuge Liang's third campaign and represented one of his few lasting territorial gains. Zhang He's death is historical: he was killed in 231 CE during a pursuit of retreating Shu forces, hit by arrows. However, there is a notable historical wrinkle: some sources suggest Sima Yi deliberately ordered Zhang He to pursue, knowing it was dangerous, because Zhang He was a potential rival for military authority. If true, Sima Yi used Zhuge Liang to eliminate a political competitor within his own ranks. The show portrays it as a Zhuge Liang ambush rather than exploring this darker possibility.

## Episode 90: The Fall of Cao Zhen

Zhuge Liang's health is visibly declining, and the physical toll of years of campaigning in harsh mountain terrain is beginning to show. Despite his condition, he orders the Shu army to withdraw from Chencang, but not before transferring all the grain stores to the Shu main depot at Qishan for future use. He calculates that the rainy season will create an opportunity.

Cao Zhen, emboldened by what he sees as another Shu retreat, divides his forces to reoccupy the abandoned positions. He takes personal command at Chencang, sending triumphant reports back to the Wei capital. His son Cao Shuang distinguishes himself in the pursuit, impressing even the Shu generals with his aggressiveness. But Cao Zhen's success is a trap. With his garrison spread thin across multiple positions, the rainy season arrives. Chencang, sitting in low ground, floods. Grain stores rot, weapons rust, and the troops suffer.

Zhuge Liang strikes at exactly the right moment, leading his forces back through the rain to assault Chencang. Cao Zhen, shocked by the reversal, desperately calls for reinforcements from his scattered garrisons, but they cannot reach him in time. The city falls. Cao Zhen is killed in the chaos of battle, thrown from his horse in the fighting. At the critical moment, Sima Yi arrives with a relief force and stabilizes the Wei lines, preventing the defeat from becoming a total collapse. Emperor Cao Rui, surveying the wreckage, has no choice but to elevate Sima Yi to Grand Commander of all Wei forces. With Cao Zhen dead, the last of the old Cao clan military leaders is gone, and Sima Yi now holds the very position that the Caos had spent years trying to deny him.

**Historical significance:** The historical Cao Zhen died of illness in 231 CE, not in battle. In the novel, Zhuge Liang sends a mocking letter that enrages Cao Zhen so severely that he dies of fury, a literary embellishment. The show takes even greater dramatic license by killing him in combat at Chencang. The real significance of Cao Zhen's death, however the series chooses to portray it, was strategic: it removed the most capable Cao family general and made Sima Yi's assumption of supreme military command inevitable. Cao Shuang, Cao Zhen's son, would later become regent, and his incompetent rule gave Sima Yi the opening to seize power in the Incident at Gaoping Tombs (249 CE), ultimately leading to the Sima family replacing the Cao dynasty entirely. This episode thus plants the seed of Wei's eventual transformation into the Jin dynasty.
