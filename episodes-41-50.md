# New Three Kingdoms (新三国) - Episodes 41-50
*From Red Cliffs to the Scramble for Jingzhou*

This batch of episodes covers the climax and aftermath of the Battle of Red Cliffs (赤壁之战), the single most consequential military engagement of the late Han period. The arc begins with the final preparations for the allied fire attack on Cao Cao's fleet, builds through the spectacular conflagration itself, and then follows the political earthquake that results: Cao Cao's humiliating retreat north, the emergence of Sima Yi as his long-term strategic advisor, and the ruthless three-way scramble between Liu Bei, Sun Quan, and Cao Cao's remnant forces over the prize territory of Jingzhou. By episode 50, Liu Bei has transformed from a homeless refugee into the master of four commanderies, setting the stage for the three-kingdom partition of China.

---

## Episode 41: Borrowing the East Wind

Cao Cao, having been tricked by Jiang Gan's theft of a forged letter in the previous episode, has already executed his two most capable naval commanders, Cai Mao and Zhang Yun. He replaces them with the veteran general Yu Jin, who orders all warships chained together with iron links to reduce rocking on the river, a fatal vulnerability the allies are counting on.

On the southern shore, Zhou Yu inspects Cao Cao's fleet from a hilltop. The wind suddenly whips his banner across his face, and the realization hits him: the entire fire attack plan is useless without a southeast wind to drive the flames northward into Cao Cao's camp. The stress causes Zhou Yu to collapse, vomiting blood. Zhuge Liang visits the bedridden commander and writes sixteen characters on a slip of paper: "To defeat Cao Cao, use fire attack; everything is ready, only the east wind is lacking." Zhou Yu is stunned that Zhuge Liang has diagnosed his exact worry. Zhuge Liang then claims he can borrow three days and three nights of southeast wind from heaven, and Zhou Yu, desperate, agrees to let him try.

Meanwhile, Cao Cao receives Huang Gai's surrender letters but remains suspicious. Only after Cai Zhong and Cai He, two planted spies, confirm that Huang Gai genuinely bears a grudge against Zhou Yu does Cao Cao accept the defection as real. On the night of the fifteenth day of the eleventh month, Cao Cao holds a grand review of his chained fleet, pours wine into the Yangtze as an offering, and composes poetry by moonlight, spear in hand, reveling in what he believes will be an easy conquest of the south.

**Historical significance:** The "borrowing the east wind" scene is pure literary invention from the novel. In actual history, the seasonal shift to southeast winds along the Yangtze in late autumn/early winter was a natural meteorological phenomenon that the allied commanders understood and waited for. The novel and this show elevate it into a quasi-supernatural feat to burnish Zhuge Liang's legend. The chaining of ships, however, likely has some historical basis: Cao Cao's northern troops suffered badly from seasickness, and some form of lashing ships together was a plausible countermeasure that backfired catastrophically when the fire attack came.

## Episode 42: The Inferno at Red Cliffs

Zhuge Liang ascends the Seven Star Altar (七星坛) to pray for the southeast wind. For three days the wind does not shift, and Zhou Yu grows suspicious, secretly ordering generals Ding Feng and Xu Sheng to kill Zhuge Liang if no wind arrives by the fifth watch. At the fourth watch, the wind suddenly changes direction to blow from the southeast. Zhuge Liang immediately flees the altar; Zhao Yun appears in a small boat to whisk him away before Zhou Yu's assassins can reach him.

Zhuge Liang races back to Jiangxia and deploys Liu Bei's forces for the coming battle. He assigns every general a task except Guan Yu, fearing that Guan Yu's old debt of gratitude to Cao Cao will lead him to spare the fleeing warlord. Guan Yu is furious at the slight and signs a military oath pledging his life that he will not let Cao Cao pass. In Cao Cao's naval camp, Huang Gai's fleet approaches as planned. At his signal, twenty warships erupt into twenty fire dragons that slam into the chained fleet. The entire northern shore becomes a wall of flame. Battle cries echo across the Three Gorges as the allied forces attack from all sides.

Cao Cao flees the inferno under relentless pursuit from Sun Quan's forces, then runs into ambushes set by Zhao Yun and Zhang Fei. By the time he reaches Huarong Road with only twenty-seven surviving men, Guan Yu's army blocks the path. Cao Cao, face streaked with tears, reminds Guan Yu of the kindness he once showed him. Guan Yu is overcome with emotion, sighs toward heaven, and orders his soldiers to stand aside. Cao Cao escapes with his life.

**Historical significance:** The Battle of Red Cliffs (208 CE) was one of the decisive battles in Chinese history. The fire attack by Huang Gai is attested in the historical records of the Sanguozhi. However, the Huarong Road encounter between Guan Yu and Cao Cao is entirely fictional, invented by the novelist Luo Guanzhong to dramatize Guan Yu's legendary loyalty and righteousness. In reality, Cao Cao retreated north through a muddy road (possibly Huarong) but was not intercepted by Guan Yu. The show follows the novel's version, making this scene one of the most emotionally charged moments in the entire series: the tension between personal honor and military duty.

## Episode 43: Cao Cao's Retreat and the Discovery of Sima Yi

In the aftermath of Red Cliffs, Sun Quan arrives at the allied camp to congratulate Zhou Yu on the great victory. Lu Su is dispatched to Liu Bei's camp to find out what happened with Cao Cao. Guan Yu returns to camp in shame, reporting that he failed to stop Cao Cao at Huarong Road. Zhuge Liang, enforcing military discipline, orders Guan Yu's execution. Liu Bei and Zhang Fei fall to their knees, begging for mercy and offering to die alongside their brother. Lu Su, moved by the scene, also speaks up in Guan Yu's defense. Zhuge Liang relents and spares Guan Yu, though the episode underscores that Zhuge Liang likely planned this outcome all along: had Cao Cao died, Sun Quan's forces would have had no northern rival to worry about and would have turned on Liu Bei immediately.

Lu Su explains to Zhou Yu the strategic logic of keeping all three factions alive: like two oxen against one tiger, neither Wu nor Liu Bei can resist Cao Cao alone, so a balance of power must be maintained.

Meanwhile, Cao Cao limps back to Nanjun (南郡), where his defeated soldiers gradually straggle in with shattered morale. Rather than despair, Cao Cao laughs heartily and tells his generals that defeat is a teacher: "A doctor who has never lost a patient learns nothing; a general who has never lost a battle understands nothing about warfare." While reviewing maps and analyzing the reasons for his defeat, Cao Cao notices an advisor named Ma Heng who has fallen asleep and is snoring loudly during the war council. Enraged, Cao Cao has him thrown in prison. Later, Cao Cao personally visits the prisoner and discovers that "Ma Heng" is actually Sima Yi (司马懿), who has been living under a false name. Cao Cao is delighted to find a mind he considers worthy of his own, and the encounter marks the beginning of Sima Yi's long ascent within Cao Cao's power structure.

**Historical significance:** The introduction of Sima Yi at this point is a significant dramatic acceleration. Historically, Sima Yi entered Cao Cao's service around 208 CE, though the exact circumstances were less theatrical than shown here. The show's decision to introduce him immediately after Red Cliffs is a brilliant structural choice: at the very moment of Cao Cao's greatest defeat, it plants the seed of the family that will eventually destroy Cao Cao's dynasty and unify China under the Jin. In real history, the Sima clan did not seize power until 265 CE, more than half a century later.

## Episode 44: Sima Yi Joins Cao Cao's Court

Cao Cao formally recruits Sima Yi to return with him to the capital at Xuchang (许昌) as a tutor and companion for his young son Cao Chong (曹冲), the child prodigy famous for weighing an elephant. Cao Cao confides his deepest reflections from the Red Cliffs defeat: he now understands that conquering the realm requires first conquering the hearts of scholars and talented men, not just winning battles. He acknowledges that his grand ambition may take two or even three generations to complete, and that without someone of Sima Yi's caliber guiding his heirs, the dream will die with him. Sima Yi, genuinely moved by this candor, accepts the appointment.

As Cao Cao prepares to march north, he receives intelligence that Ma Teng, the warlord of the western frontier (Xiliang), is planning to exploit Cao Cao's post-battle weakness by raiding Xuchang. Cao Cao conceals this alarming news from his troops to maintain morale. He fabricates a story that his third wife is about to give birth, using it as a pretext to ride ahead to Xuchang with a small escort. Once there, he orchestrates a grand, highly visible reception ceremony for himself, creating the illusion of overwhelming military strength. The bluff works: Ma Teng, seeing the apparently well-defended capital, abandons his invasion plans.

**Historical significance:** Cao Chong is a real historical figure, famous for the "weighing the elephant" anecdote recorded in the Sanguozhi. His early death (historically around 208 CE at age twelve or thirteen) was a genuine blow to Cao Cao, who reportedly said Cao Chong's death was his personal loss but his other sons' gain. Ma Teng's threat from the west was also real: the Xiliang warlords remained a persistent danger on Cao Cao's western flank. However, the show compresses and dramatizes these events. The pairing of Sima Yi with Cao Chong is the show's invention to set up the next episode's tragedy.

## Episode 45: The Death of Cao Chong

Cao Cao returns to Xuchang (shown here as the capital), where officials and his sons line up to welcome him. Cao Chong, clearly his father's favorite, is praised even by Cao Pi, the eldest surviving son, who publicly compliments his younger brother's brilliance. Sima Yi observes this family dynamic carefully and privately notes that Cao Pi, who appears to be the most obsequious and deferential of the brothers, is actually the one with the deepest cunning.

That night, Cao Chong is bitten by what appears to be a rat but is in fact a venomous snake placed deliberately in his room. The boy dies of poisoning. Cao Cao is devastated. The advisor Cheng Yu examines the wound and declares this was murder, not an accident: the bite marks are from a snake, and the dead rat was staged to make the death look natural. Cao Cao devises a test to identify the killer. He orders five cats given to each of his sons and announces that the household will hold an all-night vigil for Cao Chong, with no one permitted to sleep. By the fourth watch, every son has dozed off except one, who remains wide awake. Cao Cao recognizes that only the guilty son would be too anxious to sleep. He draws his sword, stands over the wakeful son for a long moment, then sheathes the blade. He orders Cheng Yu to bury the truth forever: this matter ends here.

After the funeral, Cao Cao summons Sima Yi and asks him to choose a new prince to serve from among the remaining sons. Sima Yi declines, saying he was so moved by his brief acquaintance with Cao Chong's intelligence that he wishes to observe three years of mourning as Cao Chong's spiritual companion. This extraordinary gesture of loyalty deeply impresses Cao Cao. In reality, Sima Yi is buying himself time to observe the succession struggle before committing to any faction.

**Historical significance:** Cao Chong's death is recorded in history but the cause is unclear; the Sanguozhi says only that he died of illness. The dramatic murder plot with the snake is the show's invention, creating a succession thriller reminiscent of palace intrigue dramas. The show never explicitly names the killer, though the scene strongly implies Cao Pi. Historically, Cao Cao did say after Cao Chong's death: "This is my misfortune but your good fortune," addressing his other sons. Sima Yi's calculated neutrality in the succession struggle is historically consistent with his known pattern of extreme patience and strategic waiting.

## Episode 46: The Contest for Jingzhou Begins

With the post-battle dust settling, Liu Bei finds himself homeless once again. Despite his contributions to the Red Cliffs victory, he controls no territory. On a quiet evening he weeps over his predicament, and Zhang Fei bluntly suggests they simply seize Nanjun by force. Zhuge Liang has a more elegant plan: let Sun Quan's forces do the hard fighting against Cao Ren's garrison at Nanjun, then swoop in and take the prize after both sides have exhausted themselves.

Sun Quan is feasting with his generals when news arrives that Liu Bei has moved his army to Youjiang Kou (油江口), dangerously close to Nanjun. Zhou Yu is alarmed and rides personally to Liu Bei's camp to assess the threat. Inspecting the camp, he notices that Liu Bei has no siege equipment such as scaling ladders, which leads Zhou Yu to conclude that Liu Bei must have an insider within Nanjun rather than plans for a direct assault. During a tense verbal exchange, Zhuge Liang goads Zhou Yu into a rash declaration: "If I cannot take Nanjun, you are free to take it yourself!" This spoken promise, made before witnesses, becomes the legal and moral basis for Liu Bei's subsequent seizure of the territory.

Zhou Yu's initial assault on Nanjun goes well, and his forces advance rapidly. But Zhuge Liang reads the situation differently, predicting that Cao Ren is employing a "feigning weakness" strategy to lure Zhou Yu into a trap. This sets the stage for the next episode's confrontation.

**Historical significance:** The contest over Jingzhou after Red Cliffs is one of the most important geopolitical struggles of the Three Kingdoms period. In real history, the division of Jingzhou between Sun Quan and Liu Bei was negotiated (with Liu Bei eventually "borrowing" the territory from Sun Quan), rather than stolen through the kind of trickery shown here. The novel and this show dramatize the split as a battle of wits between Zhuge Liang and Zhou Yu, turning a political negotiation into a series of humiliations for Zhou Yu. This portrayal, while entertaining, is widely considered unfair to the historical Zhou Yu, who was a generous and magnanimous leader according to the Sanguozhi.

## Episode 47: Zhou Yu's Trap Backfires at Nanjun

Cao Ren springs his ambush. He feigns retreat to lure Zhou Yu's forces into Nanjun's outer wall, then seals the gate and attacks from the walls above. In the chaos of the "jar city" (瓮城) trap, Zhou Yu is struck by an arrow and gravely wounded. The Eastern Wu army suffers a serious defeat and falls back.

Zhou Yu, refusing to accept the setback, devises a daring counter-strategy: he fakes his own death. The Wu camp goes into mourning with a convincing funeral display. Zhuge Liang, observing from a distance, analyzes the signs and concludes that Zhou Yu is not actually dead but is baiting Cao Ren into a night attack on the supposedly leaderless Wu camp. Sure enough, Cao Ren takes the bait and leads a night raid against Zhou Yu's position, only to find himself caught in an ambush. Zhou Yu springs from his coffin to command the counterattack, and Cao Ren's forces are routed.

But when Zhou Yu's victorious army races to Nanjun to claim the city, they find Liu Bei's flags already flying from the walls. While Zhou Yu and Cao Ren were busy destroying each other, Zhuge Liang had dispatched a swift cavalry force under Guan Yu to slip into the now-undefended Nanjun. Zhou Yu, seeing that the prize he fought and bled for has been snatched away, lets out a scream of rage; his arrow wound reopens and he falls from his horse. He swears he will reclaim Jingzhou to avenge this, the greatest humiliation of his life.

**Historical significance:** The Battle of Nanjun (Jiangling) was a real year-long siege (208-209 CE) in which Zhou Yu fought Cao Ren for control of the strategically vital city on the Yangtze. The historical battle was grueling and costly for both sides. Zhou Yu did suffer an arrow wound during the fighting, which is historically attested. However, the "fake death" stratagem and Zhuge Liang's theft of the city are novelistic inventions designed to make Zhuge Liang appear as the supreme mastermind. In reality, Zhou Yu won Nanjun through hard-fought military campaigning, and Liu Bei obtained his territory in Jingzhou through a subsequent political agreement, not a clever theft.

## Episode 48: Zhou Yu Retreats to Chaisang

The political fallout from Nanjun is severe. Sun Quan, learning that Liu Bei has seized the territory his own forces fought to win, is enraged. He launches an attack on Hefei (合肥) to the north, partly as a genuine military operation against Cao Cao and partly as a pretext to recall Zhou Yu's forces eastward.

Lu Su, ever the diplomat, recognizes that the alliance between Sun Quan and Liu Bei must be preserved despite this betrayal. He visits Zhou Yu's camp and urges him to bring his army east to reinforce Sun Quan at Hefei. Zhou Yu refuses, fixated on recovering Jingzhou. Lu Su then travels to Liu Bei's camp to demand the return of the territory. Zhuge Liang and Liu Bei engage Lu Su in a verbal sparring match, deploying legal arguments and emotional appeals. They eventually reach a compromise: Liu Bei will "borrow" Jingzhou temporarily and return it once he secures his own territorial base elsewhere. Lu Su, knowing this promise will likely never be honored, nevertheless accepts it as the best deal available and returns to persuade Zhou Yu that he has a face-saving resolution.

Zhou Yu, swallowing his fury, finally orders his army to march east to support Sun Quan. He withdraws to Chaisang (柴桑) to recuperate from his wounds.

Meanwhile, inside Jingzhou, Liu Bei and Zhuge Liang begin the campaign to consolidate their new position. They set their sights on the four southern commanderies: Lingling (零陵), Guiyang (桂阳), Wuling (武陵), and Changsha (长沙). The first target is Lingling, whose general Xing Daorong rashly charges out to fight and is captured. Zhuge Liang sees through Xing Daorong's unreliable character and uses him as a double agent to seize the city from within.

**Historical significance:** The "borrowing of Jingzhou" (借荆州) is one of the most famous episodes in Three Kingdoms lore and became a Chinese proverb: "Liu Bei borrowing Jingzhou, borrowing without returning" (刘备借荆州，有借无还). Historically, Sun Quan did cede part of Jingzhou to Liu Bei, but this was a strategic calculation rather than the result of being outmaneuvered. Lu Su genuinely advocated for the Sun-Liu alliance and saw territorial concessions as a worthwhile price for keeping Liu Bei as a buffer against Cao Cao. The show's portrayal of Lu Su as a well-meaning but somewhat naive intermediary, while the novel's tradition, understates the sophistication of his grand strategy.

---

## Episode 49: Zhao Yun Takes Guiyang

The campaign to secure the four southern commanderies of Jingzhou continues. Zhang Fei is dispatched to take Wuling, which falls quickly. The main dramatic focus is on Zhao Yun's expedition against Guiyang.

At the walls of Guiyang, the defending general Chen Ying rides out to challenge Zhao Yun in single combat. Zhao Yun, wielding his legendary Qinggang Sword (青釭剑), defeats Chen Ying decisively but spares his life, sending him back into the city. Chen Ying, deeply impressed by Zhao Yun's martial prowess and chivalry, persuades the governor Zhao Fan to surrender without further bloodshed. Zhao Fan opens the city gates and welcomes Liu Bei's forces.

However, Zhao Fan's surrender conceals a trap. Acting on the advice of his counselor Bao Long, Zhao Fan attempts to get Zhao Yun drunk at a banquet and then proposes that they ally together to defect to Cao Cao. Zhao Yun sees through the scheme. With the help of Chen Ying, who has now genuinely switched his loyalty, Zhao Yun subdues the conspirators and secures Guiyang for Liu Bei.

With three of the four commanderies now in hand, Guan Yu arrives from Jingzhou and demands the honor of taking the final one: Changsha. Zhuge Liang, knowing exactly how to motivate Guan Yu, uses reverse psychology, expressing doubt about whether Guan Yu can handle the assignment. Guan Yu, pride stoked, pledges to take Changsha or die trying.

**Historical significance:** The conquest of the four southern commanderies (Lingling, Guiyang, Wuling, and Changsha) is briefly mentioned in the Sanguozhi as occurring after Liu Bei established himself in Jingzhou, but the historical record provides almost no details about how each was taken. The individual adventures of Zhao Yun at Guiyang, Zhang Fei at Wuling, and Guan Yu at Changsha are almost entirely novelistic inventions by Luo Guanzhong, designed to give each of Liu Bei's great generals a showcase moment. The historical Zhao Fan did surrender Guiyang, and there is a tradition that he offered his widowed sister-in-law in marriage to Zhao Yun, who refused, though the details vary between sources.

## Episode 50: The Battle of Changsha

Guan Yu arrives at Changsha with his army and faces the city's most formidable defender: Huang Zhong (黄忠), an aging but extraordinarily skilled warrior. The two generals meet in single combat outside the city walls. On the first day, they fight to a draw, each recognizing the other's exceptional ability. Guan Yu gains the upper hand and knocks Huang Zhong from his horse but, moved by respect for the old warrior's courage, lets him go rather than striking the killing blow.

The next day they fight again. Huang Zhong, a master archer, has the opportunity to kill Guan Yu with a well-aimed shot but instead deliberately aims for Guan Yu's helmet tassel, repaying the mercy shown to him the previous day. When Huang Zhong returns to the city, the governor Han Xuan (韩玄) is suspicious. Having witnessed Huang Zhong spare Guan Yu twice, Han Xuan concludes that the old general is secretly colluding with the enemy. Han Xuan, a cruel and impulsive ruler, orders Huang Zhong dragged out for execution.

At the critical moment, Wei Yan (魏延), a low-ranking officer who has long despised Han Xuan's tyranny, leads a mutiny. He cuts down Han Xuan, rescues Huang Zhong, and opens the city gates to welcome Guan Yu's army. Huang Zhong, though saved, initially refuses to serve Liu Bei out of loyalty to his slain lord. Liu Bei and Zhuge Liang visit him personally, treating him with great sincerity and respect. Moved by their genuine conduct, Huang Zhong finally agrees to join Liu Bei's cause, adding one of the finest warriors of the age to Liu Bei's growing roster of talent.

With all four southern commanderies secured, Liu Bei now controls a substantial territorial base for the first time in his career. The homeless wanderer has become a regional power, and the three-way contest for China is truly underway.

**Historical significance:** The duel between Guan Yu and Huang Zhong at Changsha is one of the most beloved scenes in the Romance of the Three Kingdoms, though it is entirely fictional. Historically, Huang Zhong was already serving as a regional military officer in Changsha when Liu Bei took control of the area; he submitted along with the rest of the commandery's administration. The dramatic elements of the duel, the reciprocal mercy, Han Xuan's paranoia, and Wei Yan's mutiny are all novelistic embellishments. Wei Yan's introduction here is important for the long arc of the story: the novel repeatedly hints that Wei Yan is untrustworthy (Zhuge Liang famously claims to see a "mark of rebellion" on the back of his skull), setting up a tragic trajectory that plays out decades later. Historically, Wei Yan was a competent and loyal general whose execution after Zhuge Liang's death was a political murder, not the suppression of a genuine rebel.
